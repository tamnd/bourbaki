Given that \( A \) and \( \Rightarrow AB \) are relations in the theory \( \mathscr{T} \), we need to show that \( B \) is a relation in \( \mathscr{T} \). Here, \( \Rightarrow AB \) denotes the assembly \( \vee \neg A B \), as specified (see the Remark after CF5: \( \Rightarrow AB \) represents \( \vee \neg A B \)). Since \( \Rightarrow AB \) is a relation, it must be constructed through condition (c) of formative constructions, which requires both constituents \( \neg A \) and \( B \) to be relations. Given \( A \) is a relation, \( \neg A \) is a relation by CF2, so the first component \( \neg A \) is confirmed as a relation. However, for the disjunction \( \vee \neg A B \) to be valid, the second component \( B \) must also be a relation because both components of a disjunction must be second-species assemblies (relations).

To confirm that \( B \) cannot be a term, suppose for contradiction that \( B \) is a term (first species). A term is an assembly of the first species, and by Exercise 4, the assembly \( AB \) (with \( A \) a relation and \( B \) a term) cannot be a term or a relation. However, constructing \( \Rightarrow AB \) as \( \vee \neg A B \) directly involves \( B \) as the second component. Since \( B \) is a term (first species), this would violate condition (c) of formative constructions, as only relations (second species) can be components of disjunctions. Thus, \( B \) cannot be a term. Therefore, \( B \) must itself be a relation; this follows directly from the definitions of formative constructions and criteria CF1 and CF2, combined with Exercise 4 to exclude \( B \) as a term.
