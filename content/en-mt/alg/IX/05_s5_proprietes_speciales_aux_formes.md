## § 5. Special Properties of Alternating Bilinear Forms

### 1. Reduction of Alternating Bilinear Forms.

#### Theorem 1 {#alg-ix-s5-thm-1 .statement}

Let $A$ be a (commutative) principal ring, $E$ a free $A$-module of finite dimension $n$ and $\Phi$ an alternating bilinear form on $E$. Then there exists a basis $(e_i)_{1 \leq i \leq n}$ of $E$ and an even integer $2r \leq n$, such that

$1^o$ $\Phi(e_1, e_2) = \alpha_1, \Phi(e_3, e_4) = \alpha_2, \ldots, \Phi(e_{2r-1}, e_{2r}) = \alpha_r$

where the $\alpha_i$ are elements $\neq 0$ of $A$, and where $\alpha_i$ divides $\alpha_{i+1}$ for $i = 1, \ldots, r-1$.

$2^o$ All the other elements $\Phi(e_i, e_j)$ where $i \leq j$ are zero.

The ideals $A \alpha_i$ ($i = 1, \ldots, r$) are uniquely determined by the preceding conditions. The submodule $E^0$ of $E$ orthogonal to $E$ is generated by $e_{2r+1}, \ldots, e_n$.

We shall proceed by induction on the dimension $n$ of $E$. The theorem is evident for $n = 0$. If $\Phi = 0$, the theorem is evident also; we may therefore suppose $\Phi \neq 0$. Let $f$ be the linear mapping $d_\Phi$ of $E$ into $E^*$ associated on the right with $\Phi$ ($\S 1$, no 1); then $f(E)$ is a non-zero submodule of the module $E^*$, which is a free module of dimension $n$. Let $A\alpha_1$ be the largest invariant factor of $f(E)$ with respect to $E^*$ (chap. VII, $\S 4$, no 2, th. 1); we know (*loc. cit.*) that there exists a basis $(e'_1, a'_2, \ldots, a'_n)$ of $E^*$ and an element $f(e_2) \in f(E)$ such that $f(e_2) = \alpha_1 e'_1$. Let $(e_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n)$ be the basis of $E$ (identified with the bidual $E^{**}$) dual to $(e'_1, a'_2, \ldots, a'_n)$; we have

$$
\Phi(e_1, e_2) = -\Phi(e_2, e_1) = \langle e_1, f(e_2) \rangle = \alpha_1.
$$

Let $P$ be the submodule $Ae_1 + Ae_2$ of $E$. We shall see that $E$ is *direct sum* of $Ae_1, Ae_2$ and the orthogonal submodule $P^0$ of $P$. For this, it is enough to prove that, for every $x \in E$, there exist elements $\xi_1, \xi_2$ of $A$, uniquely determined, such that $x - \xi_1 e_1 - \xi_2 e_2 \in P^0$, that is to say such that

$$
\Phi(e_1, x - \xi_1 e_1 - \xi_2 e_2) = 0, \qquad \Phi(e_2, x - \xi_1 e_1 - \xi_2 e_2) = 0.
$$

According to (1) these conditions are written

$$
\langle e_1, f(x) \rangle = \xi_2 \alpha_1, \langle e_2, f(x) \rangle = -\xi_1 \alpha_1.
$$

But we know (*loc. cit.*) that the image of $f(E)$ by every linear form on $E^*$ is contained in the ideal $A\alpha_1$, in other words all the values $\Phi(x, y) = \langle x, f(y) \rangle$ belong to $A\alpha_1$; hence the existence and uniqueness of $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$. Thus $P^0$ is a free module of rank $n-2$; there exists therefore, in $P^0$, according to the induction hypothesis, a basis $(e_3, e_4, \ldots, e_n)$ satisfying the conditions of the statement. To prove that the basis $(e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ of $E$ thus obtained also satisfies these conditions, it is enough to prove that $\alpha_1$ divides $\alpha_2$; now this follows from the fact that all the values $\Phi(x, y)$ are multiples of $\alpha_1$. It is then clear that $e_{2r+1}, \ldots, e_n$ generate $E^0$. Finally, if $(e'_i)$ is the dual basis of $(e_i)$, we have $f(e_{2j-1}) = -\alpha_j e'_{2j}$ and $f(e_{2j}) = \alpha_j e'_{2j}$ for $j = 1, \ldots, r$ and $f(e_k) = 0$ for $k = 2r+1, \ldots, n$; the ideals $A\alpha_1, A\alpha_1, A\alpha_2, A\alpha_2, \ldots, A\alpha_r, A\alpha_r$ are therefore the invariant factors of $f(E)$ with respect to $E^*$, which proves their uniqueness (Chapter VII, $\S 4$, No. 2, Theorem 1).

#### Corollary 1 {#alg-ix-s5-thm-1-cor-1 .statement}

*Let $A$ be a commutative field, $E$ a finite-dimensional vector space of dimension $n$ over $A$, and $\Phi$ an alternating bilinear form on E. There then exists a basis $(e_i)_{1 \leq i \leq n}$ of E and a pair integer $2r \leq n$ such that

$$
\Phi\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i e_i, \sum_{i=1}^n \eta_i e_i \right) = \sum_{j=1}^r (\xi_{2j-1} \eta_{2j} - \xi_{2j} \eta_{2j-1}).
$$

In particular $\Phi$ is of pair rank $2r$.

A basis satisfying (2) is called a symplectic basis for $\Phi$.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s5-n1-rem-1 .statement}

It should be noted that this corollary is also an immediate consequence of Proposition 3 of § 4, No. 2 and of its Corollary 1, because with the notation of this proposition, one necessarily has $H = \{0\}$ since $\Phi$ is alternating.

#### Corollary 2 {#alg-ix-s5-thm-1-cor-2 .statement}

Let A be a commutative field, E a finite-dimensional vector space of dimension n over A. For every bivector $z \in \bigwedge^2 E$, there exists a basis $(e_i)_{1 \leq i \leq n}$ of E such that

$$
z = e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_3 \wedge e_4 + \cdots + e_{2r-1} \wedge e_{2r} \quad (2r \leq n).
$$

Indeed, it is enough to note that z identifies canonically with an alternating bilinear form on $E^*$ (Chapter III, § 8, No. 2), and to apply Corollary 1 to this form.

By translating Corollary 1 into matrix language, one obtains:

#### Corollary 3 {#alg-ix-s5-thm-1-cor-3 .statement}

Let $A$ be a commutative field, $R$ an alternating square matrix over $A$. The rank of $R$ is an even number $2r$, and there exists an invertible matrix $P$ over $A$ such that

$$
{}^tP.R.P = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & I_r & 0 \\
-I_r & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s5-n1-rem-2 .statement}

If $A$ is any commutative ring and $R$ an alternating square matrix of odd order n over $A$, one has det $R = 0$. This results from Cor. 3 when $A$ is a field. A direct proof in the case where $A$ is a field of characteristic $\neq 2$ is as follows: since ${}^tR = -R$, one has det $R = \det {}^tR = (-1)^n \det R$, whence $2 \det {}^tR = 0$. Now, since the determinant of an alternating matrix $(\alpha_{ij})$ is a polynomial with integer coefficients with respect to the $\alpha_{ij}$ such that $i < j$, the principle of extension of algebraic identities (Chapter IV, § 2, No. 5, Scholium) shows that our assertion is then true for an arbitrary commutative ring $A$.

### 2. Pfaffian of an alternating matrix.

Let $A$ be a commutative field of characteristic 0, and $R = (\alpha_{ij})$ an alternating square matrix of even order $2m$ over $A$. Denote by E the vector space $A^{2m}$, by $(e_i)$ ($i = 1, \ldots, 2m$) its canonical basis, and by e the element $e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{2m}$ of $\wedge^2 E$. The $m$-th exterior power of the bivector $u = \sum_{i < j} \alpha_{ij} e_i \wedge e_j \in \wedge^2 E$ is of the form $\alpha . e$, where $\alpha$ is an element of $A$ which we shall calculate. The element $\wedge^m u$ is a sum of terms of the form
$$
\alpha_{h_1 k_1} \alpha_{h_2 k_2} \cdots \alpha_{h_m k_m} e_{h_1} \wedge e_{k_1} \wedge e_{h_2} \wedge e_{k_2} \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{h_m} \wedge e_{k_m}
$$
with $h_j < k_j$ for $j = 1, \ldots, m$. Such a term is zero if two equal $e_j$ occur in it, that is to say if the set $\{ h_1, k_1, \ldots, h_m, k_m \}$ is not exactly $\{ 1, 2, \ldots, 2m \}$. Moreover, if, in (3), one simultaneously exchanges $e_{h_r}$ and $e_{h_{r+1}}$ on the one hand, $e_{k_r}$ and $e_{k_{r+1}}$ on the other hand, the product does not change; it therefore does not change under any permutation performed on the pairs $(h_1, k_1), \ldots, (h_m, k_m)$. Consider then the sets (and not the sequences) $S = \{ (h_1, k_1), \ldots, (h_m, k_m) \}$ of pairs $(h_j, k_j)$ such that $1 \leq h_j < k_j \leq 2m$ for $j = 1, 2, \ldots, m$; let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of these pairs. For $S \in \mathcal{S}$, put
1°) $\varepsilon(S) = 0$ if $\{ h_1, k_1, \ldots, h_m, k_m \} \neq \{ 1, 2, \ldots, 2m \}$;
2°) in the contrary case, $\varepsilon(S) = 1$ or $\varepsilon(S) = -1$ according as the permutation which maps $h_j$ onto $2j-1$ and $k_j$ onto $2j$ ($j = 1, \ldots, m$) is even or odd.

The preceding remarks then prove that $\wedge^m u$ is equal to
$$
m! \sum_{S \in \mathcal{S}} \varepsilon(S) (\prod_{(h, k) \in S} \alpha_{hk}) e.
$$

Introduce then $m(2m-1)$ indeterminates $X_{hk}$ indexed by means of the pairs $(h, k)$ such that $1 \leq h < k \leq 2m$, and call P the polynomial over $\mathbf{Z}$ with respect to the $X_{hk}$, defined by
$$
P((X_{hk})) = \sum_{S \in \mathcal{S}} \varepsilon(S) (\prod_{(h, k) \in S} X_{hk}).
$$

One therefore has

(6) $\bigwedge^m u = m! \mathrm{P}((\alpha_{hk})) . e.$

#### Definition 1 {#alg-ix-s5-def-1 .statement}

Given an alternating matrix $R = (\alpha_{ij})$ $(i, j = 1, \ldots, 2m)$ of even order $2m$ over an arbitrary commutative ring $A$, the pfaffian of $R$, denoted by $\mathrm{Pf}(R)$, is the element $\mathrm{P}((\alpha_{hk}))$ of $A$, where $1 \leq h < k \leq 2m$.

#### Example {#alg-ix-s5-n2-exa-1 .statement}

Suppose:

$\alpha_{12} = -\alpha_{21} = \beta_1, \alpha_{34} = -\alpha_{43} = \beta_2, \ldots, \alpha_{2m-1, 2m} = -\alpha_{2m, 2m-1} = \beta_m,$

all the other $\alpha_{ij}$ being zero (cf. th. 1). Then the pfaffian of $R = (\alpha_{ij})$ is $\beta_1 \beta_2 \ldots \beta_m$.

#### Proposition 1 {#alg-ix-s5-prop-1 .statement}

Let $R$ be an alternating matrix of even order $2m$ over a commutative ring $A$, and let $P$ be a square matrix of order $2m$ over $A$. We have

(7) $\mathrm{Pf}({}^t P . R . P) = (\det P) \mathrm{Pf}(R).$

Indeed, suppose first that $A$ is a field of characteristic 0 and put $R = (\alpha_{ij}), P = (\beta_{st})$. Associate with $R$ the bivector

$$ u = \sum_{i < j} \alpha_{ij} e_i \wedge e_j = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{1 \leq i, j \leq 2m} \alpha_{ij} e_i \wedge e_j $$

of $\bigwedge^2 \mathbf{A}^{2m}$, where $(e_i)$ denotes the canonical basis of $\mathbf{A}^{2m}$; consider ${}^t P$ as the matrix, with respect to the basis $(e_i)$, of an endomorphism $f$ of $\mathbf{A}^{2m}$. Then the bivector $(\bigwedge f)(u)$ is associated with the matrix ${}^t P . R . P$ since it is equal to $\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j, s, t} \beta_{is} \alpha_{ij} \beta_{jt} e_s \wedge e_t$. Since the extension $\bigwedge f$ of $f$ to the exterior algebra $\bigwedge \mathbf{A}^{2m}$ is an endomorphism of this algebra (chap. III, § 5, no 9), we have $\bigwedge ((\bigwedge f)(u)) = (\bigwedge f)(\bigwedge u)$; since $\bigwedge f$ is the homothety of ratio $\det f$, it follows therefore from (6) and def. 1, that we have $m! \mathrm{Pf}({}^t P . R . P) = m! (\det P) \mathrm{Pf}(R)$, whence (7) in the case under consideration. The general case follows by observing that the two members of (7) are polynomials with integer coefficients in the elements of the matrices $R$ and $P$ (chap. IV, § 2, no 5, Scholium).

#### Proposition 2 {#alg-ix-s5-prop-2 .statement}

For every alternating matrix $R$ of even order $2m$ over a commutative ring $A$, one has
$$
\det R = (\mathrm{Pf}(R))^2.
$$
Indeed, since the two members of (8) are polynomials with integer coefficients with respect to the elements of $R$, the principle of prolongation of algebraic identities (chap. IV, § 2, No. 5, Scholium) shows that it is sufficient to give the proof in the case where $A$ is a field of characteristic 0 and where $\det R \neq 0$. If $P$ is an invertible square matrix of order $2m$ over $A$, one has $\det (^tP . R . P) = (\det P)^2 (\det R)$ and $\mathrm{Pf} (^tP . R . P) = (\det P) \mathrm{Pf}(R)$ (prop. 1), so that it is sufficient to prove (8) for $^tP . R . P$ instead of $R$. According to cor. 1 of th. 1, by a suitable choice of $P$, one can suppose that the matrix $^tP . R . P$ is of the form $(\alpha_{ij})$ where
$$
\alpha_{12} = -\alpha_{21} = 1, \ldots, \alpha_{2m-1, 2m} = -\alpha_{2m, 2m-1} = 1,
$$
all the other $\alpha_{ij}$ being zero (cf. Example). Now the determinant of this matrix is equal to 1, and its Pfaffian also; this completes the proof.

### 3. Symplectic group.

Suppose that the ring $A$ is commutative. If $\Phi$ is an alternating bilinear form on $E$, the automorphisms of the module $E$ leaving $\Phi$ invariant are called the *symplectic automorphisms* (or *symplectic transformations*) relative to $\Phi$, and they form a group which is called the *symplectic group* associated with $\Phi$; it is sometimes denoted by $\mathbf{Sp}(\Phi)$.

Consider in particular, on the module $E = A^{2m}$, the alternating bilinear form $\Phi_0$ whose matrix with respect to the canonical basis $(e_i)$ of $E$ is
$$
R_m = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_m \\ -I_m & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
The symplectic automorphisms and the symplectic group relative to $\Phi_0$ are simply called symplectic automorphisms and symplectic group *in $2m$ variables* (over $A$); this group is denoted by $\mathbf{Sp}(2m, A)$ or $\mathbf{Sp}_{2m}(A)$. Every matrix $A$ of a symplectic automorphism with respect to the canonical basis $(e)_i$ is called a *symplectic matrix*. Such a matrix is invertible, and, according to formula (48) of § 1, No. 10, satisfies the relation

$$(9)$$
$$
{}^tA . R_m . A = R_m.
$$

Conversely, if a square matrix $A$ of order $2m$ over $A$ satisfies (9), it is symplectic: it is sufficient indeed to prove that it is invertible; now (9) implies $\mathrm{Pf}(R_m) = \mathrm{Pf}({}^tA . R_m . A) = (\det A) \mathrm{Pf}(R_m)$ according to prop. 1 of No. 2, hence $\det A = 1$. We have at the same time proved the following proposition:

#### Proposition 3 {#alg-ix-s5-prop-3 .statement}

*The determinant of a symplectic matrix is equal to 1.*

If $A$ is a commutative field and $\Phi$ an alternating non-degenerate bilinear form on a vector space $E$ of even dimension $2m$ over $A$, the symplectic group associated with $\Phi$ is *isomorphic* to $\mathbf{Sp}(2m, A)$, according to cor. 1 of th. 1.

b) Now prove that every bivector $x \in \overset{2}{\wedge} E$ can be written in the form $\lambda \Gamma + x_1$, where $\lambda$ is a scalar and $x_1$ a linear combination of decomposable totally isotropic bivectors. (Reduce to the case where $x$ is decomposable, and observe that if $(e_i)$ is a symplectic basis, $(e_1 + e_2) \wedge (e_{m+1} - e_{m+2})$ is totally isotropic).

c) If $p \leq m$, now prove that every $p$-vector $z \in \overset{p}{\wedge} E$ can be written $z = x \wedge \Gamma + z_1$, where $x$ is a $(p-2)$-vector and $z_1$ a linear combination of decomposable totally isotropic $p$-vectors. (Reduce to the case where $z$ is decomposable, and reason by induction on $p$. Thus reduce to the case where, $(e_i)$ being a symplectic basis, one has $z = e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge \ldots \wedge e_{p-1} \wedge e_{m+p-1}$, and consider the bivectors $(e_{p-1} + e_{p+i}) \wedge (e_{m+p-1} - e_{m+p+i})$ for $0 \leq i \leq m-p$.

d) For $1 \leq p \leq m$, now prove that every $(m+p)$-vector $z \in \overset{m+p}{\wedge} E$ can be written $z = y \wedge \Gamma^p$, where $y$ is an $(m-p)$-vector. (Reduce to the case where $z$ is decomposable; if $2r$ is the dimension of a supplement of $V_z \cap V_z^0$ with respect to $V_z$, distinguish two cases, according as $r = p$ or $r > p$; in the second case, reason by induction on $r$, in the same way as in c)). Deduce that the mapping $y \to y \wedge \Gamma^p$ of $\overset{m+p}{\wedge} E$ into $\overset{m+p}{\wedge} E$ is bijective (observe that the two spaces have the same dimension). If $y$ is a decomposable $(m-p)$-vector, show that $z = y \wedge \Gamma^p$ is decomposable and that $V_z = V_y^0$.

e) Deduce from d) that, for $p \leq m$, the subspace $\overset{p}{\wedge} E$ is the direct sum of the homogeneous component of degree $p$ of the two-sided ideal $c$ generated by $\Gamma$ in $\wedge E$, and of the subspace $R_p$ of the $p$-vectors $z_1$ such that $z_1 \wedge \Gamma^{m-p+1} = 0$ (for $z \in \overset{p}{\wedge} E$, apply d) to the $(2m-p+2)$-vector $z \wedge \Gamma^{m-p+1}$). Using c), proving that $R_p$ is generated by the totally isotropic decomposable $p$-vectors, and using d), show that the mapping $x \to x \wedge \Gamma$ of $\overset{p-2}{\wedge} E$ into $\overset{p}{\wedge} E$ is injective, and that $R_p$ has dimension $\binom{2m}{p} - \binom{2m}{p-2}$.

f) Now prove that if $p \leq m$, a necessary and sufficient condition for a $p$-vector $z$ to be of the form $x \wedge \Gamma$ is that, for every totally isotropic decomposable $m$-vector $u$, one have $z \wedge u = 0$. (Now prove that, if this condition is satisfied, and if $z \in R_p$, one has $z \wedge y = 0$ for every $(2m-p)$-vector $y$; put $y$ in the form $x \wedge \Gamma^{m-p}$, where $x$ is a $p$-vector, and then apply c) to $x$, and observe that, if $x_1$ is a totally isotropic decomposable $p$-vector, $x_1 \wedge \Gamma^{m-p}$ is a decomposable $(2m-p)$-vector which can be written $u_1 \wedge v_1$, where $u_1$ is a totally isotropic decomposable $m$-vector).

Let $\alpha$ be the annihilator of the ideal $c$ in $\wedge E$; $\alpha$ is the direct sum of the subspaces $R_m, R_{m-1} \wedge \Gamma, \ldots, R_1 \wedge \Gamma^{m-1}$ and $K \Gamma^m$. Now prove that the annihilator of $\alpha$ in $\wedge E$ is equal to $c$ (cf. § 2, exerc. 4 b)).

g) For every symplectic automorphism $u$ of $E$ (for $\Phi$), let $\bar{u}$ be the canonical extension of $u$ to an automorphism of the algebra $\wedge E$ (chap. III, § 5, no 9). Now prove that the only elements of $\wedge E$ invariant under all the automorphisms $\bar{u}$ are the linear combinations of $1, \Gamma, \Gamma^2, \ldots, \Gamma^m$ with coefficients in $K$. (If $(e_i)$ is a symplectic basis, write that an element of $\wedge E$ is invariant under the symplectic transvections (exerc. 11) corresponding to the hyperplanes orthogonal to the $e_i$; then consider the symplectic transformations $u_{ij}$ defined by $u_{ij}(e_i) = e_j, u_{ij}(e_j) = e_i, u_{ij}(e_{m+i}) = e_{m+j}, u_{ij}(e_{m+j}) = e_{m+i}, u_{ij}(e_k) = e_k$ for every other index $k$).

14) Let $A$ be a commutative field of characteristic 2, $E$ the vector space $A^{2m}$; we identify the symplectic group $Sp(2m, A)$ with the group of symplectic matrices $U$, satisfying therefore the relation $^tU . R . U = R$, where $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_m \\ I_m & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Put $D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ I_m & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; now prove that every symplectic matrix $U$ such that $I + U$ is invertible can be written in one and only one way in the form $(^tD + S)^{-1}(D + S)$, where $S$ is a symmetric matrix such that $D + S$ is invertible, and conversely (cf. § 4, exerc. 11).

15) Let $A$ be a commutative field, $E$ a vector space of dimension $2m$ over $A$, $\Phi$ a non-degenerate alternating bilinear form on $E$.
a) Extend to the endomorphisms $u$ of $E$ such that $u^*u = uu^*$ ($u^*$ being the adjoint of $u$ for $\Phi$) the results of § 4, exerc. 12 a) and b).

b) Suppose that $u^* = u$. With the notations of exerc. 12 of § 4, show that if $M$ is a minimal element of the set $\mathfrak{M}$ of non-isotropic subspaces contained in $G(p, p)$ and stable under $u$, then either $M$ is an indecomposable submodule of $E_u$, or it is the direct sum of two such isomorphic submodules (reason as in exerc. 12 c) of § 4). Give examples (with $p(X) = X - 1$) showing that both cases can occur.

c) Suppose that $u^* = -u$, $A$ not being of characteristic 2. With the same notations, let $p^h$ be the minimal polynomial of the restriction of $u$ to $M$, and let $d$ be the degree of $p$. Show that if $d(h-1)$ is odd, $M$ is an indecomposable submodule of $E_u$; if, on the contrary, $d(h-1)$ is even, $M$ is either indecomposable or the direct sum of two isomorphic indecomposable submodules.

d) Suppose that $u^*u = 1$ (in other words, $u \in Sp(\Phi)$). With the same notations as in c), if $p(X)$ does not divide $X^{d(h-1)} - 1$, or if $p(X) = X - 1$ and $(-1)^h \neq -1$, $M$ is an indecomposable submodule of $E_u$; otherwise, $M$ is either indecomposable or the direct sum of two isomorphic indecomposable submodules.
