## § 10. Angles

Throughout this paragraph, A denotes a commutative field of characteristic $\neq 2$, E a vector space of dimension 2 over A, and $\Phi$ a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on E.

### 1. Direct similitudes in a plane.

Let us recall (§ 6, No. 5) that a direct similitude of E is an automorphism $u$ of the vector space E such that $\Phi(u(x), u(y)) = (\det u)\Phi(x, y)$ for all $x, y$ in E.

#### Proposition 1 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-1 .statement}

Let $A(\Phi)$ be the subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}_A(E)$ generated by the direct similitudes of E.

a) The direct similitudes are the invertible elements of $A(\Phi)$. The algebra $A(\Phi)$ is a commutative algebra of degree 2 over A. When E contains no isotropic vector $\neq 0$, $A(\Phi)$ is a field, a quadratic extension of A; in the contrary case it is the direct sum of two fields isomorphic to A.

b) Let $(e_1, e_2)$ be an orthogonal basis of E; put $\alpha_i = \Phi(e_i, e_i)$ $(i = 1, 2)$ and $\delta = -\alpha_2/\alpha_1$. Then the matrices of the elements of $A(\Phi)$ with respect to this basis are the matrices of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & \delta b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ where $a \in A, b \in A$.

c) The space E is a monogeneous free $A(\Phi)$-module, generated by any non-isotropic vector.

Let us introduce, in fact, an auxiliary alternating bilinear form $B \neq 0$ on E; then B is non-degenerate. There exists therefore an endoNo. 1

morphism $\omega$ of E such that $\Phi(x, y) = B(\omega(x), y)$ for all $x, y$ in E. For every invertible endomorphism $u$ of E, one has
$$
\begin{align*}
\Phi(u(x), u(y)) &= B(\omega u(x), u(y)) = B(uu^{-1}\omega u(x), u(y)) \\
&= (\det u)B(u^{-1}\omega u(x), y);
\end{align*}
$$
therefore, in order that $u$ be a direct similitude, it is necessary and sufficient that one have
$$
(\det u)B(u^{-1}\omega u(x), y) = (\det u)\Phi(x, y) = (\det u)B(\omega(x), y)
$$
for all $x, y$ in E; since $\det u \neq 0$ and B is non-degenerate, this is equivalent to $u^{-1}\omega u = \omega$, or again to $u\omega = \omega u$. Let us take for B the alternating bilinear form whose matrix $S$ with respect to $(e_1, e_2)$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; denoting by $R$ and $W$ the matrices of $\Phi$ and of $\omega$ with respect to this basis, the relation $\Phi(x, y) = B(\omega(x), y)$ is written, by virtue of formula (47) of § 1, No. 10, $R = {}^tW.S$; writing this out shows that one has $W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \alpha_2 \\ -\alpha_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. If $\begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}$ denotes the matrix of $u$ with respect to $(e_1, e_2)$, the relation $u\omega = \omega u$ is therefore equivalent to the relations $b\alpha_2 = -c\alpha_1, a\alpha_2 = d\alpha_2, a\alpha_1 = d\alpha_1$, that is to say to $a = d$ and $c = \delta b$; this proves that the matrices of the direct similitudes are the invertible matrices of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & \delta b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ ($a, b$ in A).

Now the endomorphisms of E whose matrices are of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & \delta b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ ($a, b$ in A) form a vector subspace of dimension 2 of $\mathfrak{L}_A(E)$, generated by 1 and by the endomorphism $\omega$; since $\omega^2$ is the homothety of ratio $-\alpha_1\alpha_2$, this subspace is the subalgebra $A(\Phi)$ of $\mathfrak{L}_A(E)$ generated by the direct similarities. The direct similarities are the invertible elements of $A(\Phi)$, that is to say those whose matrices satisfy $a^2 - \delta b^2 \neq 0$. The fact that the algebra $A(\Phi)$ is commutative is evident. Let us apply to it the results of Chap. II, § 7, No. 7: if $\delta$ is not a square in A, that is to say if no nonzero vector of E is isotropic, $A(\Phi)$ is a field; if, on the contrary, $\delta$ is a square in A, that is to say if E contains isotropic vectors $\neq 0$, $A(\Phi)$ is the direct sum of two fields isomorphic to A. This proves a) and b).

Finally any nonisotropic vector of E may be taken as first vector $e_1$ of an orthogonal basis $(e_1, e_2)$ (§ 6, No. 1); therefore its transforms $u(e_1)$ by the elements $u$ of $\Lambda(\Phi)$ are the vectors of the form $ae_1 + be_2$ ($a, b$ in $\Lambda$), that is to say all the vectors of $E$, since all the matrices $\begin{pmatrix} a & \delta b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ are matrices of elements of $\Lambda(\Phi)$. In other words $E$ is a monogenic $\Lambda(\Phi)$-module, generated by any nonisotropic vector. One sees further that it is a free monogenic $\Lambda(\Phi)$-module, since $u(e_1) = ae_1 + be_2 = 0$ implies $a = b = 0$, hence $u = 0$. This proves c).

#### Remark 1 {#alg-ix-s10-n1-rem-1 .statement}

Let $\nu$ be the similarity of matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \delta \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with respect to $(e_1, e_2)$; the similarity $\omega$ introduced in the proof of Prop. 1 is equal to $-\alpha_1 \nu$; one has $\nu^2 = \delta$. The multiplier of the direct similarity $u = a + b \nu$ ($a, b$ in $\Lambda$) is equal to the determinant of its matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & \delta b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$, that is to say to $a^2 - \delta b^2 = (a + b \nu)(a - b \nu) = u . \overline{u}$, denoting by $\overline{u}$ the conjugate of $u$ in the algebra $\Lambda(\Phi)$ (Chap. II, § 7, No. 7); in other words the multiplier of $u$ is the norm $N(u)$ of $u$ in the algebra $\Lambda(\Phi)$ (ibid.). In particular, in order that a direct similarity $u$ be a rotation, it is necessary and sufficient that $N(u) = 1$; in order that $u$ be a homothety, it is necessary and sufficient that $u \in \Lambda^*$.

#### Remark 2 {#alg-ix-s10-n1-rem-2 .statement}

The direct similitudes $u = a + b \nu$ ($a, b$ in $\Lambda$, $b \neq 0$) whose square is a homothety are the homotheties and the scalar multiples $b \nu$ of $\nu$, since $(a + b \nu)^2 = (a^2 + \delta b^2) + 2ab \nu$. The latter are none other than the automorphisms of the vector space $E$ which *transform every vector into a vector orthogonal to it*; in fact, the matrix of such an automorphism is necessarily of the form $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & c \\ d & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ ($c, d$ in $\Lambda$), and the condition that the vector $\lambda e_1 + \mu e_2$ be transformed into an orthogonal vector is then written $\lambda \mu (d \alpha_2 + c \alpha_1) = 0$.

#### Remark 3 {#alg-ix-s10-n1-rem-3 .statement}

It is easily verified that, for $x, y$ in $E$ and $u \in \Lambda(\Phi)$, one has $\Phi(u(x), y) = \Phi(x, \overline{u}(y))$. Thus the *adjoint* endomorphism of a direct similitude $u$ is the direct similitude $\overline{u}$ *conjugate* of $u$ in $\Lambda(\Phi)$.

#### Remark 4 {#alg-ix-s10-n1-rem-4 .statement}

Since every *inverse similitude* of $E$ is the product of a direct similitude and the symmetry with respect to $Ae_1$, the matrices No. 1

of inverse similitudes with respect to $(e_1, e_2)$ are the matrices of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & -\delta b \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix}$.

Henceforth we shall denote by $S$ the group of similitudes of $E$, by $S^+$ that of direct similitudes, by $H$ that of homotheties $\neq 0$, and by $O^+$ that of rotations. Recall that one has $H \subset S^+ (§ 6, n° 5)$.

#### Corollary 1 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-1-cor-1 .statement}

*The group $S^+$ of direct similitudes is commutative. Whatever the non-isotropic vectors $x, y$ of $E$, there exists one and only one direct similitude $u$ such that $y = u(x)$.*

The first assertion follows from the fact that the algebra $A(\Phi)$ is commutative. Since $E$ is a monogenic free $A(\Phi)$-module generated by $x$ (resp. $y$), there exists one and only one element $u$ (resp. $u'$) of $A(\Phi)$ such that $y = u(x)$ (resp. $x = u'(y)$); hence $x = u(u'(x))$, and $uu'$ is the identity; this shows that $u$ is invertible, and is therefore a direct similitude.

#### Corollary 2 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-1-cor-2 .statement}

*The group $O^+$ of rotations is commutative. Whatever the vectors $x, y$ of $E$ such that $\Phi(x, x) = \Phi(y, y) \neq 0$, there exists one and only one rotation $u$ such that $y = u(x)$.*

The first assertion follows from Corollary 1. The latter also shows that there exists one and only one direct similarity $u$ such that $y = u(x)$; since $\Phi(u(x), u(x)) = \Phi(x, x)$, the multiplier of $u$ is equal to 1, and $u$ is therefore a rotation.

#### Corollary 3 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-1-cor-3 .statement}

*The group $S^+/H$ is commutative. It operates on the set of non-isotropic lines of $E$. Whatever the non-isotropic lines $D, D'$ of $E$, there exists one and only one element $\varphi$ of $S^+/H$ such that $D' = \varphi(D)$.*

This follows from Corollary 1 and from the fact that $H$ leaves every line of $E$ globally invariant.

#### Proposition 2 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-2 .statement}

*The kernel of the canonical homomorphism of $O^+$ into $S^+/H$ is $\{1, -1\}$.*

In fact 1 and $-1$ are the only elements of $H \cap O^+$.

#### Proposition 3 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-3 .statement}

The homomorphism $u \to u/\overline{u} = u^2 / N(u)$ of $S^+$ into itself has $H$ as kernel, and defines, by passing to the quotient, an isomorphism of $S^+/H$ onto $O^+$.

In fact the relation $u/\overline{u} = 1$ is equivalent to $u = \overline{u}$, that is to say to $u \in A^* = H$; hence $H$ is the kernel of $u \to u/\overline{u}$. Since $N(u/\overline{u}) = 1$, $u/\overline{u}$ is a rotation (Remark 1). It remains to prove that every element $\varphi$ of $O^+$ is of the form $u/\overline{u}$ ($u \in S^+$). If $1 + \varphi$ is invertible, one may take $u = 1 + \varphi$, since the relation $N(\varphi) = \varphi \overline{\varphi} = 1$ implies $1 + \varphi = \varphi(1 + \overline{\varphi})$. Otherwise, one has $N(1 + \varphi) = (1 + \varphi)(1 + \overline{\varphi}) = 0$, that is to say, on putting $\varphi = a + b\omega$ ($a \in A,\ b \in A,\ \omega^2 = \delta \in A$), $2(1 + a) = 0$, whence $a = -1$; now the relations $a = -1$ and $N(\varphi) = a^2 - \delta b^2 = 1$ imply $b = 0$, whence $\varphi = -1$; since $\overline{\omega} = -\omega$, it is enough, in this case, to take $u = \omega$.

When $A(\Phi)$ is a field, Proposition 3 is a particular case of Hilbert's theorem (Chapter V, § 11, No. 5, Theorem 3).

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s10-n1-cor-1 .statement}

Let us denote by $i : O^+ \to S^+/H$ and $d : S^+/H \to O^+$ the homomorphisms defined in Propositions 2 and 3, and let us write the abelian groups $O^+$ and $S^+/H$ additively; then $d(i(\theta)) = 2\theta$ for $\theta \in O^+$
and $i(d(\varphi)) = 2\varphi$ for $\varphi \in S^+/H$.

In fact, if $\overline{\varphi}$ is a rotation, we have $\overline{\varphi} = \varphi^{-1}$, whence $d(i(\varphi)) = \varphi/\overline{\varphi} = \varphi^2$. On the other hand, if $\varphi \in S^+/H$, $\varphi$ is the class mod. $H$ of a direct similarity $u$, and $d(\varphi) = u/\overline{u} = u^2 / N(u)$ is congruent to $u^2$ mod. $H$; whence the second formula.

### 2. Plane trigonometry.

In this No. we shall choose a generator $\omega$ of the algebra $A(\Phi)$ such that $\omega^2 \in A$. Such a generator is determined up to a homothety (No. 1, Remark 2), therefore the element $\omega^2$ of $A$, which we shall denote by $\delta$, is determined modulo the multiplicative group $(A^*)^2$ of squares of non-zero elements of $A$.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s10-n2-rem-1 .statement}

When –1 belongs to the class mod. $(A^*)^2$ in question, one generally chooses $\omega$ in such a way that $\omega^2 = -1$, which determines it up to sign. When this class contains 1 but not –1, one generally chooses $\omega$ in such a way that $\omega^2 = 1$, which again determines it up to sign.

#### Proposition 4 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-4 .statement}

a) When $\omega^2 = \delta$ is not a square in $A$ (that is to say, when $E$ does not contain any isotropic right), the mapping $t$ of $S^+/H$ into $\tilde{A}$ is a bijection.

b) When $\delta$ is the square of an element $\gamma$ of $A$, the mapping $t$ is a bijection of $S^+/H$ onto $\tilde{A}$ with the elements $1/\gamma$ and $-1/\gamma$ removed.

c) Denoting $S^+/H$ additively, one has, for $\varphi, \varphi'$ in $S^+/H$

(2)
$$
t(\varphi + \varphi') = (t(\varphi) + t(\varphi'))/(1 + \delta t(\varphi)t(\varphi'))
$$
when $t(\varphi)$ and $t(\varphi')$ are finite and $1 + t(\varphi)t(\varphi')$ is $\neq 0$.

In fact, since $S^+/H$ is a set of lines (with 0 omitted) of $A(\Phi)$ considered as a vector plane over $A$, $t$ is injective. On the other hand, for an element $a + b\omega$ ($a \in A, b \in A$) of $A(\Phi)$ to be a direct similitude, it is necessary and sufficient that it be invertible, that is to say, that one have $N(a + b\omega) = a^2 - \delta b^2 \neq 0$, or again $(b/a)^2 \neq 1/\delta$; this proves the assertions of surjectivity in a) and b). #### Proposition 6 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-6 .statement}

*Let d be the isomorphism of $S^+/H$ onto $O^+$ defined in Prop. 3. For every element $\varphi$ of $S^+/H$ such that $t = t(\varphi)$ is finite, one has*

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s10-n2-cor-1 .statement}

*For every element $\theta$ of $O^+$ such that $t(\theta)$ is finite, one has*

(7)

$$
*For every element $\varphi$ of $S^+/H$ such that $t(\varphi)$ is finite and $1 + \delta t(\varphi)^2 \neq 0$, one has*

In fact this follows immediately from Prop. 6 and from the corollary to Prop. 3.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s10-n2-rem-2 .statement}

Formulas (6) remain true for $t = \infty$ provided one replaces the rational functions occurring on the right-hand side by their canonical extensions to the projective field $\tilde{A}$ (Chapter II, 2nd ed., App. III, No. 5); in fact, if $t = \infty$, $\varphi$ is the class of $\omega$, and one has $d(\varphi) = -1, s(d(\varphi)) = 0$ and $c(d(\varphi)) = -1$; these are indeed the values taken by the canonical extensions of the right-hand sides for $t = \infty$. The same is true for (7) when $t(\theta) = \infty$, and for (8) when $t(\varphi) = \infty$ or when $1 + \delta t(\varphi)^2 = 0$. Analogously, formula (2) remains true when only one of the elements $t(\varphi), t(\varphi'), t(\varphi)$, for example, is infinite, provided one considers its right-hand side as a rational function of $t(\varphi)$ alone: in fact the product of the similarities $1 + t(\varphi')\omega$ and $\omega$ is $\delta t(\varphi') + \omega$, whereas the value taken by the canonical extension of the right-hand side of (2) is $1/\delta t(\varphi')$. Finally, when $t(\varphi)$ and $t(\varphi')$ are finite and one has $1 + \delta t(\varphi)t(\varphi') = 0$, one has $t(\varphi) + t(\varphi') = 0$ (otherwise $t(\varphi)^2$ would be equal to $1/\delta$, which is impossible (prop. 4)); one may therefore agree that the value of the right-hand side of (2) is $\infty$, and this value is indeed that of the left-hand side. When $t(\varphi)$ and $t(\varphi')$ are both infinite, the right-hand side of (2) is not defined.

### 3. Angles.

In this No. and the next, we shall suppose that A is an ordered field, hence of characteristic zero. Recall (Rectifications to Chapter VI) that, if F is a vector space over A, the relation "there exists $\lambda > 0$ such that $y = \lambda x$" is an equivalence relation between elements $x, y$ of $F - \{ 0 \}$, that every equivalence class for this relation is called an open half-line with origin 0, and that the union of an open half-line and of $\{ 0 \}$ is called a closed half-line (or simply half-line) with origin 0; if D is a line and $\Delta$ a closed half-line contained in D, D is the union of $\Delta$ and of $-\Delta$, and contains no other closed half-line. We shall say that a half-line is isotropic if the line containing it is isotropic.

Recall also (loc. cit.) that, given a finite-dimensional vector space of dimension $n$ over A, an orientation on F is the datum of one of the two half-lines of the space $\wedge^n F$; the $n$-vectors belonging to this half-line are said to be positive. A finite-dimensional vector space endowed with an orientation is said to be oriented.

The homotheties of E whose ratio is $> 0$ obviously form a subgroup of index 2 of H; we shall denote it by $H^+$. The canonical homomorphism $i : O^+ \to S^+/H$ (cf. prop. 2) is the composite of the canonical homomorphisms of $S^+/H^+$ onto $S^+/H$ and of $O^+$ into $S^+/H^+$; since $O^+ \cap H^+ = \{ 1 \}$, this latter homomorphism is injective.

#### Proposition 7 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-7 .statement}

Suppose that A is a maximal ordered field and that $\Phi$ is a positive form (§ 7). Then the canonical homomorphisms of $O^+$ into $S^+/H^+$ and of $O^+/\{ 1, -1 \}$ into $S^+/H$ are bijective, and $S^+$ is isomorphic to $O^+ \times H^+$.

We have already seen that the homomorphisms in question are injective, and it is enough to prove that the first is surjective. Let $(e_1, e_2)$ be an orthonormal basis of E, and let $\omega$ be the direct similarity such that $\omega(e_1) = e_2$ (cor. 1 of prop. 1, No. 1); one then has $\omega^2 = -1$ (prop. 1, b)). Given an arbitrary direct similarity u = a + bw (a \in A,\ b \in A),\ \text{one has}\ N(u) = a^2 + b^2 > 0,\ \text{and there exists one and only one rotation contained in the same half-line of } A(\Phi) \text{ as } u, \text{ namely } (a^2 + b^2)^{1/2}\ u.\ \text{QED.}

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s10-n3-cor-1 .statement}

*Given two half-lines* D, D' *with origin* 0, *there exists one and only one rotation* v *such that* v(D) = D'.

The hypotheses imply in fact that E contains no isotropic lines. Our assertion then follows from cor. 1 of prop. 1 (No. 1).

Henceforth we shall suppose that A is a *maximal ordered field*, and that the form $\Phi$ is *positive*. In the set of pairs $(D_1, D_2)$ of lines (resp. half-lines with origin 0) of E, the relation “there exists a direct similitude (resp. a rotation) u such that $u(D_1) = D'_1$ and $u(D_2) = D'_2$” is an equivalence relation between the pairs $(D_1, D_2)$ and $(D'_1, D'_2)$. The equivalence class of the pair $(D_1, D_2)$ is called, by definition, *the angle of the lines* (resp. *half-lines*) $D_1, D_2$ (taken in this order); it is denoted by $(\widehat{D_1, D_2})$.

#### Proposition 8 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-8 .statement}

*Suppose that* A *is a maximal ordered field*, and that the *form* $\Phi$ *is positive*. *Let* $D_1, D_2, D'_1, D'_2$ *be four lines* (resp. *half-lines*) *with origin* 0 *of* E. *A necessary and sufficient condition that the angles* $(\widehat{D_1, D_2})$ *and* $(\widehat{D'_1, D'_2})$ *be equal is that the angles* $(\widehat{D_1, D'_1})$ *and* $(\widehat{D_2, D'_2})$ *be equal*.

Let us prove the necessity of the stated condition. Let u be a direct similitude (resp. a rotation) such that $u(D_1) = D'_1$ and $u(D_2) = D'_2$. There exists, by cor. 3 of prop. 1, a direct similitude (resp. by the cor. of prop. 7, a rotation) v such that $v(D_1) = D_2$. Since the group $S^+$ (resp. $O^+$) is *commutative*, we have $D'_2 = u(v(D_1)) = v(u(D_1)) = v(D'_1)$, and this shows that $(\widehat{D_1, D'_1}) = (\widehat{D_2, D'_2})$. The sufficiency is deduced from the necessity by interchanging $D_2$ and $D'_1$.

It follows from prop. 8 that to every angle $(\widehat{D_1, D_2})$ of lines (resp. half-lines) with origin 0 of E, there is canonically associated a well determined element of $S^+/H$ (resp. $O^+$), namely the class mod. H of the direct similitudes $\varphi$ (resp. the rotation $\varphi$) such that $u(D_1) = D_2$ for any representative $(D_1, D_2)$ of the angle $(\widehat{D_1, D_2})$. Thus there has been defined a canonical bijection $h$ (resp. $h'$) of the set $\mathfrak{A}_0$ of angles of lines (resp. $\mathfrak{A}$ of angles of half-lines) onto $S^+/H$ (resp. $O^+$); in particular, for every $\varphi \in \mathfrak{A}$, one says that $h(\varphi)$ is the rotation of angle $\varphi$. We shall transport to $\mathfrak{A}_0$ and $\mathfrak{A}$, by means of $h^{-1}$ and of ${h'}^{-1}$, the structures of commutative groups of $S^+/H$ and of $O^+$, and we shall denote additively the groups $\mathfrak{A}_0$ and $\mathfrak{A}$ thus obtained. If $D, D', D''$ denote lines (resp. half-lines) with origin 0 of E, we have by definition

$$
(9) \quad (\widehat{D, D''}) = (\widehat{D, D'}) + (\widehat{D', D''}) \tag{relation de Chasles};
$$

whence one deduces

$$
(10) \quad (\widehat{D, D}) = 0, \qquad (\widehat{D, D'}) = - (\widehat{D', D}).
$$

#### Remark 1 {#alg-ix-s10-n3-rem-1 .statement}

The set L of lines (resp. half-lines) with origin 0 of E is a homogeneous space for the abelian group $S^+/H$ (resp. $O^+$) such that the identity element is the only operator leaving invariant all the elements of L. One may therefore apply to L the formulas of Chapter II, 2nd ed., App. II, No. 1; prop. 8 is thus a particular case of the “parallelogram rule”, and formulas (9) and (10) particular cases of formulas (2) (loc. cit.).

#### Remark 2 {#alg-ix-s10-n3-rem-2 .statement}

In the definition of the group of angles of lines, one may, instead of the group $S^+/H$, use the group $O^+/ \{ -1, 1 \}$ which is canonically isomorphic to it (Prop. 7). #### Proposition 9 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-9 .statement}

Suppose that the field $\mathbf{A}$ is maximal ordered, and that $\Phi$ is a positive form. For every integer $n > 1$, the number of elements $\theta$ of the group $\mathfrak{A}_0$ of angles of lines (resp. $\mathfrak{A}$ of angles of half-lines) such that $n\theta = 0$ is equal to $n$.

Since $\mathfrak{A}_0, S^+/H, \mathfrak{A}$ and $O^+$ are isomorphic (Prop. 3), it is enough to give the proof for $O^+$, that is to say, to show that there are exactly $n$ rotations $\varphi$ such that $\varphi^n = 1$. Now, since $\mathbf{A}$ is a maximal ordered field and $\mathbf{A}(\Phi)$ is an extension field of degree 2 of $\mathbf{A}$ (Prop. 1 a)), $\mathbf{A}(\Phi)$ is an algebraically closed field (Chapter VI, § 2, No. 6, Theorem 3). Hence the $n$-th roots of unity in $\mathbf{A}(\Phi)$ form a cyclic group of order $n$ (Chapter V, § 11, No. 1, Theorem 1). Since one has $N(u) = u \overline{u} \geqslant 0$ for every $u \in \mathbf{A}(\Phi)$, the relation $u^n = 1$ implies that one has $N(u) = 1$, hence that $u$ is a rotation (No. 1, Remark 1). This proves our assertion.

#### Lemma 1 {#alg-ix-s10-lem-1 .statement}

Let $u$ be a direct similitude of $E$; all the bivectors of the form $x \wedge u(x)$ belong to the same closed half-line of $\overset{2}{\wedge} E$.

The case where $u$ is a homothety is trivial. In the contrary case one has $x \wedge u(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \neq 0$; let $x, y$ be two vectors of $E$ ($x \neq 0, y \neq 0$); there exists $\varphi \in S^+$ such that $y = \varphi(x)$, whence $y \wedge u(y) = \varphi(x) \wedge u \varphi(x) = \varphi(x) \wedge \varphi(u(x)) = (\det \varphi)(x \wedge u(x))$; taking an orthonormal basis of $E$, one sees that $\det \varphi$ is positive (Proposition 1 b)); whence our assertion.

This being so, among the two generators $\omega$ of $\mathbf{A}(\Phi)$ such that $\omega^2 = -1$, there exists one and only one such that the bivector $x \wedge \omega(x)$ is positive for all $x \in E$. It is this generator that we shall choose in order to define the functions $c_w, s_w$ and $t_w$ (No. 2). Let $h$ and $h'$ be the canonical bijections defined above from the group $\mathfrak{A}_0$ of angles of lines onto $S^+/H$ and from the group $\mathfrak{A}$ of angles of half-lines onto $O^+$. The composite mappings $t_w \circ h$ of $\mathfrak{A}_0$ into the projective field $\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}$, $c_w \circ h'$ and $s_w \circ h'$ of $\mathfrak{A}$ into the field $A$ are denoted respectively by $\operatorname{tg}$, $\cos$ and $\sin$, and are called the *tangent*, *cosine* and *sine functions*. The mapping $\varphi \to 1/\operatorname{tg} \varphi$ of $\mathfrak{A}_0$ into $\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}$ is denoted by $\operatorname{cotg}$ and is called the *cotangent function*. One says that the sine, tangent and cotangent functions are the *trigonometric functions*. The composite mappings $\operatorname{tg} \circ p$ and $\operatorname{cotg} \circ p$, where $p$ denotes the canonical homomorphism of $\mathfrak{A}$ onto $\mathfrak{A}_0$ (Remark 2 above), are again denoted by $\operatorname{tg}$ and $\operatorname{cotg}$ by abuse of language.

Formulas (2), (8), (3), (4), (5) and (7) of No. 2 give, since one has here $\delta = -1$

$$
\begin{align*}
(11) \quad & \operatorname{tg} (\varphi + \varphi') = (\operatorname{tg} \varphi + \operatorname{tg} \varphi')/(1 - \operatorname{tg} \varphi \operatorname{tg} \varphi') \\
(12) \quad & \operatorname{tg} (2\varphi) = 2 \operatorname{tg} \varphi/(1 - \operatorname{tg}^2 \varphi)
\end{align*}
$$

for $\varphi, \varphi'$ in $\mathfrak{A}_0$;

$$
\begin{align*}
(13) \quad & \cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta = 1 \\
(14) \quad & \cos (\theta + \theta') = \cos \theta \cos \theta' - \sin \theta \sin \theta' \\
(15) \quad & \sin (\theta + \theta') = \sin \theta \cos \theta' + \cos \theta \sin \theta' \\
(16) \quad & \left\{ \begin{array}{l}
\sin (2\theta) = 2 \operatorname{tg} \theta/(1 + \operatorname{tg}^2 \theta), \\
\cos (2\theta) = (1 - \operatorname{tg}^2 \theta)/(1 + \operatorname{tg}^2 \theta)
\end{array} \right.
\end{align*}
$$

for $\theta, \theta'$ in $\mathfrak{A}$. On the other hand one has, by definition or as an easy consequence of the preceding formulas:

\end{align*}
$$

for $\theta \in \mathfrak{A}$.

Given two non-zero vectors $x, y$ of $E$, the *angle* of these two vectors (taken in this order) is defined to be, and is denoted by $\widehat{(x, y)}$, the angle of the half-lines to which they belong. For every vector $x$ of $E$ the *length* of $x$ is defined to be, and is denoted by $|x|$, the element $\Phi(x, x)^{1/2}$ of $A$.

#### Proposition 10 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-10 .statement}

Suppose that the field $\mathbf{A}$ is maximal ordered, that the plane $\mathbf{E}$ is oriented, and that the form $\Phi$ is positive. For every pair of non-zero vectors $x, y$ of $\mathbf{E}$ one has

$$
\cos (\widehat{x, y}) = \Phi(x, y)/|x|.|y|
$$
$$
\sin (\widehat{x, y}).e = (x \wedge y)/|x|.|y|,
$$

where $e$ denotes the positive bivector such that $\Phi_{(2)}(e, e) = 1$.

In fact, since the vectors $x' = x/|x|$ and $y' = y/|y|$ both have length 1, there exists one and only one rotation $\varphi$ such that $\varphi(x') = y'$ (No. 1, Cor. 2 of Prop. 1). If one writes $\varphi = a + b \omega$ ($a, b$ in $\mathbf{A}$), then by definition one has $a = \cos (\widehat{x, y})$ and $b = \sin (\widehat{x, y})$. The relation $y' = \varphi(x') = ax' + b \omega(x')$ gives $\Phi(x', y') = a \Phi(x', x') = a$ since $x'$ and $\omega(x')$ are orthogonal (Remark 2 of No. 1); this proves (19). On the other hand this relation also gives $x' \wedge y' = bx' \wedge \omega(x') = b.e$ by the definition of the extension $\Phi_{(2)}$ of $\Phi$ to $\bigwedge^2 \mathbf{E}$ ($§ 1,$ No. 9, formula (37)) and the choice of $\omega$; this proves (20).

#### Remark 4 {#alg-ix-s10-n3-rem-4 .statement}

Let $F$ be a vector space *of arbitrary dimension* over the maximal ordered field $\mathbf{A}$, and let $\Psi$ be a positive non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on $F$. Given two linearly independent vectors $x, y$ of $F$, let $F'$ be the vector plane which they generate; the *angle* of $x$ and $y$ is defined to be the angle of $x$ and $y$ considered as elements of the plane $F'$; it is denoted by $(\widehat{x, y})$. The cosine of this angle is, by virtue of (19), given by

$$
\cos (\widehat{x, y}) = \Psi(x, y)/|x|.|y|
$$

(where $|x| = \Psi(x, x)^{1/2}$ is also called the *length* of the vector $x$), and is therefore *independent of the orientation chosen* on $F'$; the sine and the tangent of $(\widehat{x, y})$ change sign if one changes the orientation of $F'$. Given two non-zero proportional vectors $x, y$ of $F$, one sets $(\widehat{x, y}) = 0$ by convention.

### 4. Angular sectors.

We shall assume first, without any further assumption, that E is an oriented plane over the ordered field A. We shall say that three half-lines D₀, D₁, D₂ (with origin 0) of E form a direct sequence if, for xᵢ ∈ Dᵢ, xᵢ ≠ 0 (i = 0, 1, 2), at least two of the bivectors x₀ ∧ x₁, x₁ ∧ x₂, x₂ ∧ x₀ are > 0; in this case the sequences D₁, D₂, D₀ and D₂, D₀, D₁ are also direct. It is clear that three half-lines forming a direct sequence are distinct. Given two half-lines D₁, D₂ of E, the open (resp. closed) angular sector with origin D₁ and extremity D₂ is the set (or, by abuse of language, the union) of the half-lines D such that the sequence D₁, D, D₂ is direct (resp. such that D = D₁ or D = D₂ or the sequence D₁, D, D₂ is direct).

#### Proposition 11 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-11 .statement}

Let E be an oriented plane over an ordered field A, D₀ a half-line of E, and G the set of half-lines of E distinct from D₀. The relation

“D₁ = D₂, or the sequence D₀, D₁, D₂ is direct”

between elements D₁, D₂ of G is a total ordering relation in G.

Indeed the axioms for total ordering relations are verified trivially, with the exception of transitivity. Let D₁, D₂, D₃ be three half-lines such that the sequences D₀, D₁, D₂ and D₀, D₂, D₃ are direct; we shall now prove that the sequence D₀, D₁, D₃ is direct. For this let us take a vector xᵢ ≠ 0 in Dᵢ (i = 0, 1, 2, 3), choose a bivector e > 0, and set xᵢ ∧ xⱼ = aᵢⱼe (aᵢⱼ ∈ A). Writing e = x₀ ∧ y (y ∈ E), and taking (x₀, y) as a basis of E, one easily verifies the relation

$$
a_{01}a_{23} + a_{02}a_{31} + a_{03}a_{12} = 0.
$$

This being so, if a₀₁ ≤ 0, one has a₁₂ > 0 and a₂₀ > 0 (since the first sequence is direct), then a₂₃ > 0 and a₃₀ > 0 (since a₀₂ < 0 and the second sequence is direct), whence a₁₃ > 0 (by virtue of (22)); therefore the sequence (D₀, D₁, D₃) is direct in this case. Suppose henceforth that a₀₁ > 0. If a₃₀ ≤ 0, one has a₀₂ > 0 and a₂₃ > 0 (since the second sequence is direct), then a₁₂ > 0 (since a₂₀ < 0 and the first sequence is direct), whence $a_{13} > 0$ (by (22)), and the sequence $(D_0, D_1, D_3)$ is direct. Finally it is obviously analogous if $a_{01} > 0$ and $a_{30} > 0$. QED.

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s10-n4-cor-1 .statement}

*Let $D_1$ and $D_2$ be two distinct half-lines of E. For every half-line $D_0$ of E such that the sequence $D_0, D_1, D_2$ is direct, the set of half-lines $D$ of E such that $D_1 < D < D_2$ (for the total ordering relation defined by $D_0$) is equal to the open angular sector with origin $D_1$ and extremity $D_2$.

Indeed, given a half-line $D_3$, it is a question of proving that the relations “the sequence $D_1, D_3, D_2$ is direct” and “the sequences $D_0, D_1, D_3$ and $D_0, D_3, D_2$ are direct” are equivalent. For brevity let us denote by $(ijk)$ the relation “the sequence $(D_i, D_j, D_k)$ is direct”. By prop. 11, the conjunction of (132) and (120) implies (130); analogously the conjunction of (201) and (213) implies (203); whence one half of our assertion. Conversely suppose (012), (013), and (032); since the conjunction of (312) and (320) implies (310) (prop. 11), and since (310) and (013) are incompatible, (312) is false; this proves (132) and completes the proof.

By virtue of the preceding corollary, the open (resp. closed) angular sector with origin $D_1$ and extremity $D_2$ is denoted by $\{D_1, D_2\}$ (resp. $[D_1, D_2]$), it being understood that these are intervals for the order structure defined by any half-line $D_0$ such that the sequence $D_0, D_1, D_2$ is direct.

#### Proposition 12 {#alg-ix-s10-prop-12 .statement}

*Let A be a maximal ordered field, E an oriented plane over A, $D_0$ a half-line of E, and G the set of half-lines of E distinct from $D_0$. The totally ordered sets A and G (prop. 11) are isomorphic.

Let, indeed, $(x, y)$ be a basis of E such that $x \in - D_0$ and the bivector $x \wedge y$ be $> 0$. To every element $t$ of A let us correspond the half-line $f(t)$ to which the vector $(1 - t^2)x + 2ty$. It is clear that $f(A) \subset G$. Let us prove that $f$ is strictly increasing. Indeed, in order that the sequence $D_0, f(t), f(t')$ ($t, t'$ in A) be direct, it is necessary and sufficient, by definition, that at least two of the elements

$$
-2t, \quad (1-t^2)2t' - (1-{t'}^2)2t, \quad 2t'
$$

let them be $> 0$. Now the second is equal to $2(t' - t)(1 + tt')$. Hence, if $t < t'$, then either $tt' \geqslant 0$, whence $t' > 0$ or $-t > 0$, or $tt' < 0$, whence $-t > 0$ and $t' > 0$; in any case $D_0, f(t), f(t')$ is direct. Since $A$ is totally ordered, $f$ is an isomorphism of $A$ onto the ordered set $f(A)$ (*Ens*, Chapter III, § 1, No. 14, Prop. 13).

It remains to prove that $f$ is *surjective*. For this, consider the positive form $\Phi$ on $E$ such that $(x, y)$ is an orthonormal basis for $\Phi$. For every half-line $D \in G$, there exists an angle $\varphi$ and only one such that $2\varphi = (\widehat{-D_0, D})$ (No. 1, Prop. 3); since $(\widehat{-D_0, D})$ is not the straight angle, $\varphi$ is not the right angle, and $\operatorname{tg} \varphi$ is therefore finite. Then, by virtue of formulas (16) (No. 3), we have $D = f(\operatorname{tg} \varphi)$. This completes the proof.
