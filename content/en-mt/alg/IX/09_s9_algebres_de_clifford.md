## § 9. Clifford Algebras

In this paragraph, we shall suppose that the ring $\mathbf{A}$ is commutative. We shall denote by $Q$ a quadratic form on the $\mathbf{A}$-module $E$, and by $\Phi$ the associated bilinear form ($§\ 3$, No. 4).

### 1. Definition and universal property of the Clifford algebra.

#### Definition 1 {#alg-ix-s9-def-1 .statement}

The Clifford algebra of $Q$, denoted by $C(Q)$, is the quotient algebra of the tensor algebra $T(E)$ of the module $E$ by the two-sided ideal (denoted by $I(Q)$) generated by the elements of the form $x \otimes x - Q(x)\cdot 1$ $(x \in E)$.

We shall denote by $\rho_Q$ (or simply by $\rho$ when there is no danger of confusion) the mapping of $E$ into $C(Q)$ composed of the canonical mapping of $E$ into $T(E)$ and the canonical mapping $\sigma$ of $T(E)$ onto $C(Q)$; the mapping $\rho_Q$ is said to be canonical. Let us note that $C(Q)$ is generated by $\rho_Q(E)$, and that, for $x \in E$, we have

$$
\rho(x)^2 = Q(x)\cdot 1;
$$

whence, replacing $x$ by $x + y$ ($x,\ y$ in $E$)

$$
\rho(x)\rho(y) + \rho(y)\rho(x) = \Phi(x,\ y)\cdot 1
$$

#### Example {#alg-ix-s9-n1-exa-1 .statement}

If $E$ admits a basis consisting of a single element $e$, $T(E)$ is isomorphic to the polynomial algebra $\mathbf{A}[X]$, and $C(Q)$ is a quadratic extension of $\mathbf{A}$, having as basis $(1,\ u)$, where $u$ is the element $u = \rho(e)$ and satisfies $u^2 = Q(e)$.

Let $T^h$ denote the $h$-th tensor power $\bigotimes^h E$ in $T(E)$, and let $T^+$ (resp. $T^-$) be the sum of the $T^h$ for $h$ even (resp. odd).

Comme $T(E)$ is the direct sum of $T^+$ and $T^-$ and $I(Q)$ is generated by elements of $T^+$, $I(Q)$ is the direct sum of $T^+ \cap I(Q)$ and $T^- \cap I(Q)$, and $C(Q)$ is the direct sum of the two submodules $C^+(Q) = \sigma(T^+)$ and $C^-(Q) = \sigma(T^-)$ (also denoted by $C^+$ and $C^-$). The elements of $C^+$ will be called even (resp. odd). We have the relations

(3) $C^+C^+ \subset C^+, \quad C^+C^- \subset C^-, \quad C^-C^+ \subset C^-, \quad C^-C^- \subset C^+$.

In particular $C^+$ is a subalgebra of $C(Q)$.

#### Proposition 1 {#alg-ix-s9-prop-1 .statement}

*Let f be a linear mapping of E into an algebra D over A such that $f(x)^2 = Q(x).1$ for all $x \in E$. There exists one and only one homomorphism $\bar{f}$ of $C(Q)$ into D such that $f = \bar{f} \circ \rho_Q$.

The uniqueness of $\bar{f}$ follows from the fact that $C(Q)$ is generated by $\rho_Q(E)$. Let $h$ be the unique homomorphism of $T(E)$ into D which extends $f$ ($h$ is defined by $h(x_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes x_n) = f(x_1) \ldots f(x_n)$). We have

$$
h(x \otimes x - Q(x).1) = (f(x)^2 - Q(x)).1 = 0,
$$

and consequently $h$ vanishes on $I(Q)$ and defines by passing to the quotient the homomorphism $\bar{f}$ in question.

Prop. 1 expresses that $C(Q)$ is the solution of a universal mapping problem (*Sets*, Chap. IV, § 3, No. 1).

Take in particular for D the opposite algebra of $C(Q)$ and for $f$ the mapping $\rho$; prop. 1 entails that there exists one and only one anti-automorphism $\beta$ of $C(Q)$ whose restriction to $\rho(E)$ is the identity; it is called the *principal* antiautomorphism of $C(Q)$. It is clear that $\beta^2 = 1$.

D’autre part let $Q'$ be a quadratic form on an A-module $E'$ and $f$ a linear mapping of $E$ into $E'$ such that $Q' \circ f = Q$. We have $\rho_Q(f(x))^2 = Q'(f(x)).1 = Q(x).1$, and consequently there exists one and only one homomorphism $C(f)$ of $C(Q)$ into $C(Q')$ such that $C(f) \circ \rho_Q = \rho_{Q'} \circ f$. If $f$ is the identity, $C(f)$ is the identity; if $Q''$ is a quadratic form on an A-module $E''$ and $g$ a linear mapping of $E'$ into $E''$ such that $Q'' \circ g = Q'$, we have $C(g \circ f) = C(g) \circ C(f)$. When $E'$ is a submodule of $E$ and $f$ the canonical injection of $E'$ into $E$ (so that $Q'$ is the restriction of $Q$ to $E'$), we say that $C(f)$ is the *canonical* homomorphism of $C(Q')$ into $C(Q)$.

Let us take in particular $Q' = Q$ and for $f$ the mapping $x \to -x$; we see that there exists one and only one automorphism $\alpha$ of $C(Q)$ such that $\alpha \circ \rho = -\rho$; it is called the principal automorphism of $C(Q)$. It is clear that $\alpha^2 = 1$, and that the restriction of $\alpha$ to $C^+$ (resp. $C^-$) is the identity (resp. the mapping $u \to -u$).

#### Proposition 2 {#alg-ix-s9-prop-2 .statement}

Let $A'$ be a commutative ring, $\varphi$ a homomorphism of $A$ into $A'$, $Q’$ the quadratic form on $E' = A' \otimes_A E$ deduced from $Q$ by extension of scalars ($\S 3$, no 4, prop. 3). There exists one and only one isomorphism $j$ of the algebra $A' \otimes_A C(Q)$ onto $C(Q')$ such that $j (1 \otimes \rho_Q(x)) = \rho_{Q'}(1 \otimes x)$ for every $x \in E$.

It suffices to prove that the algebra $C' = A' \otimes C(Q)$ and the mapping $1 \otimes \rho_Q$ of $E'$ into $C'$ form a solution of the same problem of universal mapping as $C(Q')$ and $\rho_{Q'}$. Now, let D’ be an algebra over $A'$ and let $f'$ be an $A'$-linear mapping of $E'$ into D’ such that $f'(x')^2 = Q'(x').1$ for every $x' \in E'$. The mapping $g : x \to f' (1 \otimes x)$ of E into D’ (considered as an A-module by means of the homomorphism $\varphi$) is A-linear and we have $g(x)^2 = Q' (1 \otimes x).1 = Q(x).1$ for every $x \in E$. There therefore exists one and only one A-homomorphism $\overline{g}$ of $C(Q)$ into D’ such that $\overline{g}(\rho_Q(x)) = f'(1 \otimes x)$. Consequently there exists one and only one $A'$-homomorphism $\overline{f}'$ of $C'$ into D’ such that $\overline{f}'(1 \otimes \rho_Q(x)) = f'(1 \otimes x)$ for every $x \in E$; by linearity it follows that $\overline{f}'((1 \otimes \rho_Q)(x')) = f'(x')$ for every $x' \in E'$. QED.

### 2. Some operations in the tensor algebra.

In this no we shall denote by $e_x$ ($x \in E$) the linear mapping $u \to x \otimes u$ of the tensor algebra T(E) into itself.

#### Lemma 1 {#alg-ix-s9-lem-1 .statement}

Let $f$ be an element of the dual $E^*$ of E. There exists one and only one linear mapping $i_f$ of T(E) into itself such that
$$
i_f(1) = 0 \tag{4}
$$
$$
i_f \circ e_x + e_x \circ i_f = f(x).I \tag{5}
$$
for every $x \in E$
(where I denotes the identity mapping). The mapping $f \to i_f$ of $E^*$ into $\mathcal{L}(T(E))$ is linear. We have $i_f(T^n) \subset T^{n-1}$, $(i_f)^2 = 0$, and $i_f \circ i_g + i_g \circ i_f = 0$ for $f, g$ in $E^*$. The mapping $i_f$ is zero on the subalgebra of

T(E) generated by the kernel of f. The ideal I(Q) is stable under $i_f$; passing to the quotient, $i_f$ therefore defines a linear mapping (still denoted by $i_f$) of $C(Q)$ into itself.

Indeed formula (5) can be written

$$
i_f(x \otimes u) = -x \otimes i_f(u) + f(x) \cdot u \quad (x \in E,\ u \in T(E)).
$$

As (4) completely determines $i_f$ on $T^0$, and since (6) determines $i_f$ on $T^n$ if its values on $T^{n-1}$ are known, the uniqueness of $i_f$ is proved. On the other hand, for $x \in E$ and $u \in T^{n-1}$, the right-hand side of (6) is bilinear on $E \times T^{n-1}$; this proves the existence of $i_f$ by induction on $n$ (Chap. III, § 1, No. 2) and also proves, by induction on $n$, that $i_f(T^n) \subset T^{n-1}$. If $f = ag + bh$ (a, b in A, g, h in E*) it is clear that $ai_g + bi_h$ satisfies (4) and (5), hence is equal to $i_f$. We have

$$
(i_f)^2 \circ e_x = -i_f \circ e_x \circ i_f + f(x)i_f = e_x \circ (i_f)^2
$$

and, since $(i_f)^2(1) = 0$, it follows by induction on $n$ that $(i_f)^2$ is zero on $T^n$. Replacing $f$ by $f + g$, the relation $(i_f)^2 = 0$ gives $i_f \circ i_g + i_g \circ i_f = 0$. An analogous reasoning by induction to the preceding ones shows that $i_f$ is zero on the subalgebra generated by the kernel of $f$. Finally (6) entails that the set of elements $u$ of $I(Q)$ such that $i_f(u) \in I(Q)$ is a left ideal of $T(E)$; moreover, if $u = (x \otimes x - Q(x) \cdot 1) \otimes \nu$ ($x \in E,\ \nu \in T(E)$), we have

$$
\begin{align*}
i_f(u) &= f(x)x \otimes \nu - x \otimes i_f(x \otimes \nu) - Q(x)i_f(\nu) \\
&= f(x)x \otimes \nu - f(x)x \otimes \nu + x \otimes x \otimes i_f(\nu) - Q(x)i_f(\nu) \\
&= (x \otimes x - Q(x) \cdot 1) \otimes i_f(\nu);
\end{align*}
$$

therefore $I(Q)$ is stable under $i_f$, and the last assertion follows. QED.

Let F be a bilinear form on E; in the remainder of this paragraph we shall denote by $i_x^F$ ($x \in E$) the mapping $i_f$ corresponding to the linear form $f : y \to F(x, y)$ on E.

#### Lemma 2 {#alg-ix-s9-lem-2 .statement}

There exists one and only one linear mapping $\lambda_F$ of $T(E)$ into itself such that

$$
\begin{align*}
\lambda_F(1) &= 1 \\
\lambda_F \circ e_x &= (e_x + i_x^F) \circ \lambda_F \qquad (x \in E).
\end{align*}
$$

For any $f \in E^*$, we have

$$
\lambda_F \circ i_f = i_f \circ \lambda_F.
$$

Indeed, formula (8) is equivalent to

$$
\lambda_F(x \otimes u) = x \otimes \lambda_F(u) + i_x^F(\lambda_F(u)) \quad (x \in E, u \in T(E)).
$$

Comme (7) completely determines $\lambda_F$ on $T^0$, and since (10) determines $\lambda_F$ on $T^n$ if its values on $T^{n-1}$ are known, the uniqueness of $\lambda_F$ is demonstrated. On the other hand, for $x \in E$ and $u \in T^{n-1}$, the right-hand side of (10) is bilinear on $E \times T^{n-1}$; this demonstrates the existence of $\lambda_F$ by induction on $n$. It remains to prove (9), which we shall do by induction; both members of (9) are zero on $T^0$; suppose (9) to be true on $\sum_{h=0}^{n-1} T^h$; then, for $x \in E$ and $u \in T^{n-1}$, we have:

$$
\begin{align*}
(\lambda_F \circ i_f)(x \otimes u) &= (-\lambda_F \circ e_x \circ i_f + f(x)\lambda_F)(u) \\
&= -(e_x + i_x^F) \circ \lambda_F \circ i_f(u) + f(x)\lambda_F(u) \\
&= -(e_x + i_x^F) \circ i_f \circ \lambda_F(u) + f(x)\lambda_F(u) \\
&= (i_f \circ e_x \circ \lambda_F - f(x)\lambda_F + i_f \circ i_x^F \circ \lambda_F + f(x)\lambda_F)(u) \\
&= (i_f \circ (e_x + i_x^F) \circ \lambda_F)(u) = (i_f \circ \lambda_F)(x \otimes u),
\end{align*}
$$

whence our last assertion.

#### Lemma 3 {#alg-ix-s9-lem-3 .statement}

Let F and G be two bilinear forms on E. We have $\lambda_F \circ \lambda_G = \lambda_{F+G}$. For every bilinear form F on E, $\lambda_F$ is a bijection of $T(E)$ onto itself.

Indeed, $\lambda_F \circ \lambda_G$ has the characteristic properties (7) and (8) of $\lambda_{F+G}$: we have $(\lambda_F \circ \lambda_G)(1) = 1$, and

$$
\begin{align*}
\lambda_F \circ \lambda_G \circ e_x &= \lambda_F \circ (e_x + i_x^G) \circ \lambda_G = (e_x + i_x^G + i_x^F) \circ \lambda_F \circ \lambda_G \\
&= (e_x + i_x^{F+G}) \circ \lambda_F \circ \lambda_G.
\end{align*}
$$

On the other hand, if $F = 0$, we have $i_x^F = 0$ for every $x \in E$, and consequently $\lambda_F = I$, which entails that, for every F, we have $\lambda_F \circ \lambda_{-F} = \lambda_{-F} \circ \lambda_F = I$.

### 3. Basis of the Clifford algebra.

#### Proposition 3 {#alg-ix-s9-prop-3 .statement}

Let Q and Q' be two quadratic forms and F a bilinear form on E such that $Q'(x) = Q(x) + F(x, x)$ for every $x \in E$. The mapping $\lambda_F$ maps the ideal $I(Q')$ onto the ideal $I(Q)$, and defines an isomorphism (denoted by $\overline{\lambda_F}$) of the A-module $C(Q')$ onto the A-module $C(Q)$.

Since $\lambda_F$ is a bijection whose inverse bijection is $\lambda_{-F}$ (Lemma 3), it suffices to prove the inclusion $\lambda_F(I(Q')) \subset I(Q)$. Since $I(Q)$ is a left ideal stable under $i_x^F$ (Lemma 1), (8) shows that the set of $u \in T(E)$ such that $\lambda_F(u) \in I(Q)$ is a left ideal. It therefore suffices to prove that, whatever $u \in T(E)$ and $x \in E$, we have $\lambda_F(x \otimes x \otimes u - Q'(x)u) \in I(Q)$. Now, according to (8) and Lemma 1, we have
$$
\lambda_F \circ e_x^2 = (e_x + i_x^F)^2 \circ \lambda_F = (e_x^2 + F(x, x)) \circ \lambda_F,
$$
whence
$$
\begin{align*}
\lambda_F(x \otimes x \otimes u - Q'(x)u) &= (e_x^2 + F(x, x) - Q'(x)) \circ \lambda_F(u) \\
&= (x \otimes x - Q(x)) \otimes \lambda_F(u) \in I(Q).
\end{align*}
$$

#### Lemma 4 {#alg-ix-s9-lem-4 .statement}

If the quadratic form $Q$ is zero, $C(Q)$ is none other than the exterior algebra of E.

Indeed the exterior algebra of $E$ is none other than the quotient of $T(E)$ by the two-sided ideal $J$ generated by the elements of the form $a \otimes \nu \otimes a$ ($a \in E, \nu \in T(E)$) (Chap. III, § 5, No. 5 and No. 9). It is clear that $I(Q) \subset J$. It is therefore enough to show that $a \otimes \nu \otimes a \in I(Q)$. This is obvious if $\nu \in T^0$. Suppose this assertion proved for $\nu \in \sum_{h=0}^{n-1} T^h$, and let $x \in E$ and $u \in T^{n-1}$; we have
$$
a \otimes x \otimes u \otimes a = (a + x) \otimes (a + x) \otimes u \otimes a
- a \otimes a \otimes u \otimes a - x \otimes a \otimes u \otimes a - x \otimes x \otimes u \otimes a
$$
and the four terms of the right-hand side belong to $I(Q)$.

Suppose in particular that the module $E$ admits a basis $(x_i)_{i \in L}$, and totally order the set of indices $L$. We know (Chap. III, § 5, No. 6) that the exterior algebra of $E$ admits as a basis the family formed by the elements $x_H$, where $H$ runs through the set of finite subsets of $L$ and where $x_H$ is the element $x_{h_1} \wedge \ldots \wedge x_{h_q}$, $(h_1, \ldots, h_q)$ denoting the strictly increasing sequence of the elements of $H$.

On the other hand, consider the bilinear form $F$ defined by $F(x_i, x_j) = -\Phi(x_i, x_j)$ if $i > j$, $F(x_i, x_j) = 0$ if $i < j$ and $F(x_i, x_i) = -Q(x_i)$. It is clear that $Q(x) + F(x, x) = 0$; with the notations of Prop. 3, it follows from this proposition and Lemma 4 that $\bar{\lambda}_F$ is an isomorphism of $\wedge E$ onto $C(Q)$, which is therefore a free $A$-module. Now prove, by induction on the number of elements of $H$, that we have

$$
\bar{\lambda}_F(x_H) = \rho(x_{h_1}) \ldots \rho(x_{h_q})
$$

(where $(h_1, \ldots, h_q)$ is the strictly increasing sequence of the elements of $H$). This is obvious if $H$ has 0 or 1 element. Suppose (11) verified for the subsets having at most $q - 1$ elements. Consider a subset $H$ having $q$ elements, denote its smallest element by $j$, and write $H = \{ j \} \cup K$, where $K$ is a subset having $q - 1$ elements. We have, by (8) and the induction hypothesis

$$
\bar{\lambda}_F(x_H) = \bar{\lambda}_F(x_j \wedge x_K) = \rho(x_j) \bar{\lambda}_F(x_K) + i^F_{x_j}(\bar{\lambda}_F(x_K)) = x'_H + i^F_{x_j}(x'_K),
$$

putting, for every finite subset $J$ of $L$, $x'_J = \rho(x_{j_1}) \ldots \rho(x_{j_s})$, where $(j_1, \ldots, j_s)$ is the increasing sequence of the elements of $J$. Now, for $i \in K$, $x_i$ belongs to the kernel of the linear form $y \to F(x_j, y)$, hence $i^F_{x_j}(x'_K) = 0$ (Lemma 1). This proves the desired result.

We have therefore proved the following theorem:

#### Theorem 1 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-1 .statement}

*Suppose that the $A$-module $E$ admits a basis $(x_i)_{i \in L}$, the set of indices $L$ being endowed with a total ordering. For every finite subset $H$ of $L$, put $x_H = \rho(x_{h_1}) \rho(x_{h_2}) \ldots \rho(x_{h_q})$, where $(h_1, \ldots, h_q)$ is the strictly increasing sequence of the elements of $H$. Then the elements $x_H$ form a basis of the $A$-module $C(Q)$.*

#### Corollary 1 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-1-cor-1 .statement}

*If $E$ is a free module of dimension $n$, $C(Q)$ is a free module of dimension $2^n$; moreover, if $n > 0$, $C^+$ and $C^-$ are free modules of dimension $2^{n-1}$.

This follows at once from the properties of the binomial coefficients.*

#### Corollary 2 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-1-cor-2 .statement}

*If $E$ is a free module, the canonical mapping $\rho$ of $E$ into $C(Q)$ and the mapping $a \to a.1$ of $A$ into $C(Q)$ are injective.*

#### Corollary 3 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-1-cor-3 .statement}

*Suppose that $E$ is the direct sum of two free submodules $E_1$ and $E_2$. Let $Q_i$ be the restriction of $Q$ to $E_i$ and $p_i$ the canonical mapping of $C(Q_i)$ into $C(Q)$ ($i = 1, 2$). Then the linear mapping $p$ of $C(Q_1) \otimes C(Q_2)$ into $C(Q)$ deduced from the bilinear mapping $(a, b) \to p_1(a)p_2(b)$ of $C(Q_1) \times C(Q_2)$ into $C(Q)$ is a bijection.

It is enough in fact to consider the basis of $E$ obtained by taking the union of a basis of $E_1$ and a basis of $E_2$.*

#### Corollary 4 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-1-cor-4 .statement}

The hypotheses and notation being those of Cor. 3, suppose moreover that $E_1$ and $E_2$ are orthogonal, and transport to $C(Q_1) \otimes C(Q_2)$, by means of the bijection $p$, the algebra structure of $C(Q)$. If $a_i$ and $b_i$ are even or odd elements of $C(Q_i)$ ($i = 1,\ 2$), then $a\ (a_1 \otimes a_2)(b_1 \otimes b_2) = \varepsilon(a_1b_1) \otimes (a_2b_2)$, with $\varepsilon = 1$ unless $a_2$ and $b_1$ are odd, in which case $\varepsilon = -1$.

It suffices indeed to prove that $p_2(a_2)p_1(b_1) = \varepsilon p_1(b_1)p_2(a_2)$, and for this purpose we may suppose that $p_2(a_2)$ (resp. $p_1(b_1)$) is a product $x_1 \ldots x_h$ (resp. $y_1 \ldots y_k$) of elements of $\rho_Q(E_2)$ (resp. $\rho_Q(E_1)$). Since $E_1$ and $E_2$ are orthogonal, we have

$$
x_iy_j + y_jx_i = \Phi(x_i, y_j) = 0,
$$

whence

$$
x_1 \ldots x_h y_1 \ldots y_k = (-1)^{hk} y_1 \ldots y_k x_1 \ldots x_h.
$$

The conclusions of cor. 3 and 4 remain true if we omit the hypothesis that $E_1$ and $E_2$ are free modules (cf. exerc. 1).

### 4. Structure of the Clifford algebra.

In this no., we shall assume that $A$ is a field, that $E$ is a finite-dimensional vector space of dimension $m$ over $A$, and that the quadratic form $Q$ is non-degenerate (which, according to th. 1 of § 5, no. 1, requires that $m$ be even if $A$ is of characteristic 2). Since $E$ is free, the canonical mapping $\rho$ is injective (no. 3, cor. 2 of th. 1). We shall henceforth identify $E$ with its image in $C(Q)$.

#### Theorem 2 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-2 .statement}

Suppose that the dimension of $E$ is an even number $m = 2r$ and that $Q$ is neutral (§ 4, no. 2). Then the algebra $C(Q)$ is separable (chap. VIII, § 7, no. 5, def. 1) and is isomorphic to the algebra of endomorphisms of a vector space of dimension $2^r$ over $A$. Moreover, if $m > 0$, $C^+(Q)$ is separable and is direct sum of two ideals isomorphic to the algebra of endomorphisms of a vector space of dimension $2^{r-1}$ over $A$.

Indeed, since Q is neutral, E can be decomposed into the direct sum of two totally singular subspaces N and P of dimensions r (§ 4, no. 2, cor. 1 of prop. 2). The restriction of Q to N being zero, the subalgebra S of C(Q) generated by N is identified with the exterior algebra of N (no. 3, lemma 4). For $n \in \mathbf{N}$, we shall denote by $e'_n$ the mapping $t \to nt$ of S into itself.

Let $(n_1, \ldots, n_r)$ be a basis of N; we shall denote by $(p_1, \ldots, p_r)$ the basis of P such that $\Phi(n_i, p_j) = \delta_{ij}$ (§ 4, no. 2, prop. 2). For $p \in P$, we shall denote by $p'$ the linear form $n \to \Phi(n, p)$ on N, and by $i_p$ the endomorphism of S obtained by passing to the quotient from the endomorphism $i_{p'}$ of $T(N)$ associated with $p'$ as was said in lemma 1 of no. 2. By (5), we have

$$
e'_n \circ i_p + i_p \circ e'_n = \Phi(n, p) \qquad (n \in \mathbf{N}, \ p \in \mathbf{P}).
$$

Put, for $x = n + p \in E$ (with $n \in \mathbf{N}$ and $p \in \mathbf{P}$), $s(x) = e'_n + i_p$. It is clear that s is a linear mapping of E into $\mathcal{L}(S)$. Since we have
$$
s(x)^2 = (e'_n + i_p)^2 = Q(n) + \Phi(n, p) = Q(x)
$$
by virtue of (12) and lemma 1 (no. 2), s extends to a homomorphism (which we shall still denote by s) of C(Q) into $\mathcal{L}(S)$ (no. 1, prop. 1). We shall now prove that this homomorphism is surjective, which, since C(Q) and $\mathcal{L}(S)$ are both of dimension $2^{2r}$, will imply that s is an isomorphism and will prove our first assertion.

Let us indeed denote by $I$ the interval $[1, r]$. For any subset H of I, we shall put $H' = I - H$ and denote by $n_H$ (resp. $p_H$) the product of the $n_i$ (resp. $p_i$) for $i \in H$, arranged in increasing order of the indices. Recall that the $n_H$ form a basis of S (No. 3, Theorem 1). Finally, for any two subsets H, K of I, put $x_{H, K} = n_H p_I n_K$. We shall now prove that the elements $s(x_{H, K})$ of $s(C(Q))$ generate $\mathcal{L}(S)$. Now, if $j \notin H$, we have $s(p_j)(n_H) = i_{p_j}(n_H) = 0$ by Lemma 1, since the $n_i$ for $i \in H$ belong to the kernel of the linear form $n \to \Phi(n, p_j)$ on N; on the other hand, we have
$$
s(p_j)(n_j n_H) = (i_{p_j} \circ e'_{n_j})(n_H) = \Phi(p_j, n_j)n_H - n_j \cdot s(p_j)(n_H) = n_H
$$
(by (12)). Since s is a homomorphism, it follows that, for any two subsets H, K of I, $s(p_K)(n_H) = 0$ if $K \not\subset H$, and that $s(p_K)(n_H) = \pm n_{H-K}$ if $K \subset H$. Since, for $M \subset I$ and $L \subset I$, by definition $s(n_M)(n_L) = n_M n_L$, and $n_M n_L$ is zero if $M \cap L \neq \emptyset$ and is equal to $\pm n_{M \cup L}$ otherwise, it follows from the above that, for any subsets $H, K, L$ of $I$, $s(x_{H,K})(n_L) = s(n_H)s(p_I)s(n_{K'}) (n_L)$ is zero if $K \neq L$ and is equal to $\pm n_H$ if $K = L$. This shows that the $s(x_{H,K})$ generate $\mathcal{L}(S)$ and completes the proof of the first assertion.

To prove the second assertion, put $S^+ = S \cap C^+$ and $S^- = S \cap C^-$; it is clear that $S^+$ (resp. $S^-$) is the subspace of S generated by the $n_H$ such that H has an even (resp. odd) number of elements, that S is the direct sum of $S^+$ and $S^-$, and that $s(C^+)$ leaves $S^+$ and $S^-$ stable. Consequently $s$ maps $C^+$ into a subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}(S)$, isomorphic to the product $\mathcal{L}(S^+) \times \mathcal{L}(S^-)$; the restriction of $s$ to $C^+$ is an isomorphism of $C^+$ onto this subalgebra, since $s$ is injective and $C^+$ and $\mathcal{L}(S^+) \times \mathcal{L}(S^-)$ both have dimension $2^{2r-1}$ (No. 2, Corollary 1 of Theorem 1). QED.

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s9-n4-cor-1 .statement}

*If $m$ is even, but $Q$ of arbitrary index, the algebra $C(Q)$ is a central simple algebra of dimension $2^m$. Moreover, if $m > 0$, the subalgebra $C^+(Q)$ is separable, and its center Z is of dimension 2 over A. When Z is a field, Z is a separable quadratic extension of A and $C^+(Q)$ is simple; otherwise, Z is direct product of two fields isomorphic to A, and $C^+(Q)$ is then direct product of two simple subalgebras of dimensions $2^{m-2}$.

Indeed, let $A'$ be the algebraic closure of A, and $Q'$ the quadratic form on $E' = A' \otimes_A E$ deduced from Q by extension of scalars. We have seen that $C(Q')$ is isomorphic to $A' \otimes_A C(Q)$ (prop. 2), and it is clear that $C^+(Q')$ is isomorphic to $A' \otimes_A C^+(Q)$. Since $Q'$ is neutral ($\S 4$, No. 2, Cor. 2 of prop. 3), the corollary is an immediate consequence of Th. 2 and the permanence theorems of Chap. VIII, $\S 7$.

#### Remark 1 {#alg-ix-s9-n4-rem-1 .statement}

Since the algebra $C(Q)$ is simple, it has only one class of irreducible representations; they are called *spinor representations*; when one fixes one's attention on one of these representations, say $\tau$, the elements of the space on which $\tau$ is carried out are called *spinors*. If Q is neutral, the restriction of $\tau$ to $C^+(Q)$ is, like that of $s$, the sum of two inequivalent absolutely irreducible representations; the elements of the subspaces on which these two representations are carried out are called semispinors. In the general case, if $C^+(Q)$ is not simple, the restriction of $\tau$ to $C^+(Q)$ must, since it is faithful, contain subrepresentations belonging to each of the two classes of irreducible representations of $C^+(Q)$, and hence is the sum of two inequivalent absolutely irreducible representations, since this is so after extension of scalars to the algebraic closure $A'$ of $A$. On the other hand, if $C^+(Q)$ is simple, it has only one class of irreducible representations, and the restriction of $\tau$ to $C^+(Q)$ is therefore irreducible, since it decomposes by extension of scalars to $A'$ into two non-equivalent representations.

#### Remark 2 {#alg-ix-s9-n4-rem-2 .statement}

Suppose that $A$ is of characteristic $\neq 2$, and let $(x_1, \ldots, x_m)$ ($m = 2r$) be an orthogonal basis of $E$. Put
$$
z = 2^r x_1 \ldots x_m \in C(Q);
$$
since $x_i x_j + x_j x_i = 0$ for $i \neq j$, we have $zx_j = -x_j z$, which entails that z belongs to the center $Z$ of $C^+(Q)$ without belonging to $A$. We have
$$
z^2 = 2^{2r}(-1)^r Q(x_1) \ldots Q(x_m) = (-1)^r D
$$
where D denotes the discriminant of $\Phi$ with respect to the basis $(x_j)$ (cf. exerc. 9).

#### Theorem 3 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-3 .statement}

*Suppose that the dimension of $E$ is an odd number $m = 2r + 1$ (and hence that $A$ is of characteristic $\neq 2$).*

a) *The algebra $C^+(Q)$ is central simple. If Q is of maximum index $r$, $C^+(Q)$ is isomorphic to the endomorphism algebra of a vector space of dimension $2^r$ over $A$.*

b) *The algebra $C(Q)$ is separable. Its center $Z$ is of dimension 2, and $C(Q)$ is isomorphic to $Z \otimes_A C^+(Q)$, and hence is simple or a direct sum of two simple subalgebras.*

Let $x_0$ be a non-isotropic vector of $E$, and let $F$ be the orthogonal of $x_0$; denote by $Q_1$ the quadratic form $y \to -Q(x_0)Q(y)$ on $F$; it is clear that $Q_1$ is non-degenerate. Since $x_0 y = -y x_0$ (for $y \in F$), we have $(x_0 y)^2 = -Q(x_0)Q(y) = Q_1(y)$, and consequently the mapping $y \to x_0 y$ of $F$ into $C^+(Q)$ extends to a homomorphism $h$ of $C(Q_1)$ into $C^+(Q)$ (No. 1, Prop. 1). Now $C(Q_1)$ is simple (Th. 2) and has the same dimension $2^{2r}$ as $C^+(Q)$; this implies, since $h(1) = 1$, that $h$ is an isomorphism. Moreover, if $Q$ is of index $r$, $x_0$ can be chosen in such a way that $Q_1$ is also of index $r$ (\$ 4, No. 2, Prop. 3), which proves $a$.

Soit now $(x_1, \ldots, x_{2r})$ an orthogonal basis of $F$; let $z = x_0 x_1 \ldots x_{2r}$. One verifies immediately that $z$ commutes with $x_j$ for $j = 0, \ldots, 2r$, and hence belongs to the center of $C(Q)$. Let $Z$ be the subspace of $C(Q)$ generated by $1$ and $z$; it is a subalgebra of the center of $C(Q)$ and a quadratic extension of $A$, since $z$ is odd and $z^2$ is equal to the scalar $(-1)^r Q(x_0) \ldots Q(x_{2r})$. Consider the homomorphism $\theta$ of $Z \otimes_A C^+(Q)$ into $C(Q)$ defined by $\theta(u \otimes v) = uv$. Since $z \in C^-$ and is invertible, the mapping $u \to zu$ is an isomorphism of the module $C^+$ onto $C^-$, which entails that $\theta(Z \otimes C^+)$ contains $C^+$ and $C^-$, and hence coincides with $C(Q)$. Since $Z \otimes C^+$ and $C(Q)$ have the same dimension $2^{2r+1}$, $\theta$ is an isomorphism; this proves $b$, in view of the results of Chap. VIII, § 7.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s9-n4-rem-3 .statement}

The discriminant $D$ of $\Phi$ with respect to the basis $(x_j)_{(j=0,\ldots,2r)}$ is equal to $2^{2r+1} Q(x_0) \ldots Q(x_{2r})$. Consequently $Z$ is generated by $1$ and by the odd element $z' = 2^{r+1} z$ such that $z'^2 = (-1)^r 2D$. The algebra $C(Q)$ is therefore simple if and only if $2(-1)^r D$ is not a square in $A$.

### 5. Clifford group.

In this No., we suppose that $A$ is a field, that $E$ is of finite dimension $m$, and that $Q$ is non-degenerate. We identify $E$ with its canonical image in $C(Q)$.

#### Definition 2 {#alg-ix-s9-def-2 .statement}

The Clifford group of $Q$ (resp. special Clifford group of $Q$) is called the multiplicative group of the invertible elements $s$ of $C(Q)$ (resp. $C^+(Q)$) such that $sEs^{-1} = E$.

In this No. we shall denote by $G$ and $G^+$ the Clifford group and the special Clifford group of $Q$. It is clear that $G^+ = G \cap C^+(Q)$.

#### Theorem 4 {#alg-ix-s9-thm-4 .statement}

Put, for $s \in G$ and $x \in E$, $\varphi(s).x = sx s^{-1}$.

a) The mapping $\varphi$ is a homomorphism of $G$ into the orthogonal group $O(Q)$ of $Q$ and its kernel is the set of invertible elements of the center $Z$ of $C(Q)$.

b) The set $E \cap G$ is the set of nonsingular vectors of $E$; for $x \in E \cap G$, $-\varphi(x)$ is the symmetry with respect to the hyperplane orthogonal to $x$.

c) If $\dim(E)$ is even, one has $\varphi(G) = O(Q)$, $\varphi(G^+)$ is of index 2 in $O(Q)$ if $E \neq \{0\}$, and is equal to $SO(Q)$ if $A$ is of characteristic $\neq 2$.

d) If $\dim(E)$ is odd (which implies that $A$ is of characteristic $\neq 2$), one has $\varphi(G) = \varphi(G^+) = SO(Q)$.

Indeed, one has $Q(sxs^{-1}) = (sxs^{-1})^2 = sx^2 s^{-1} = Q(x)$ for $s \in G$ and $x \in E$, which shows that $\varphi(s) \in O(Q)$. For $\varphi(s) = 1$ to hold, it is necessary and sufficient that $s$ commute with the elements of $E$, that is to say, belong to the center $Z$ of $C(Q)$. This proves a).

For an element $x$ of $E$ to belong to $G$, it is necessary that it be invertible, that is to say, that it be a nonsingular vector (since $x^2 = Q(x)$). If this is so, one has $x^{-1} = Q(x)^{-1} x$, whence, for every $y \in E$,
$$ xy x^{-1} = Q(x)^{-1} xy x = Q(x)^{-1} x (\Phi(x, y) - xy) = - (y - \Phi(x, y) Q(x)^{-1} x); $$
this proves b) (\S 6, no 4).

#### Lemma 5 {#alg-ix-s9-lem-5 .statement}

Every element $s$ of $G$ is of the form $zs'$, where $z$ is an invertible element of $Z$ and $s'$ belongs to $G \cap C^+(Q)$ or to $G \cap C^-(Q)$; the subgroup $G^+$ is of index 2 in $G$ when $E \neq \{0\}$.

The second assertion follows obviously from the first, since the nonsingular vectors belong to $G \cap C^-(Q)$. Suppose first that $\dim(E)$ is even, and let $s = t' + t''$, with $t' \in C^+(Q)$ and $t'' \in C^-(Q)$; by definition we have $sx = (\varphi(s).x)s$ for every $x \in E$; since $t'x$ and $(\varphi(s).x)t'$ (resp. $t''x$ and $(\varphi(s).x)t''$) are odd (resp. even) elements, we have $t'x = (\varphi(s).x)t'$, whence $s^{-1} t' x = xs^{-1} t'$ for every $x \in E$. It follows that $s^{-1} t' \in Z$ and, since $\dim(E)$ is even, $Z = A$ (No. 4, cor. of Theorem 2), hence $t' = as$, where $a \in A$. If $a \neq 0$, we therefore have $s = a^{-1} t'$ and $t' \in G \cap C^+(Q)$; if $a = 0$, $s = t'' \in G \cap C^-(Q)$ and the lemma is proved in this case. If $\dim(E)$ is odd, $A$ is of characteristic $\neq 2$, hence for every $s \in G$, $\varphi(s)$ is a product of symmetries with respect to nonsingular vectors $x_i (i = 1, 2, \ldots, h)$ (§ 6, No. 4, Proposition 5); if we put $s' = x_1 x_2 \ldots x_h$, we have $\varphi(s) = \varphi(s')$, hence $s = z s'$, where $z \in \mathbf{Z}$, and $s'$ belongs to $C^+(Q)$ or to $C^-(Q)$ according as $h$ is even or odd.

Suppose $\dim(E)$ is even. Since every element $u$ of $\mathbf{O}(Q)$ extends in one and only one way to an automorphism $\bar{u}$ of $C(Q)$ (Proposition 1), and since $C(Q)$ is central simple (Theorem 2), $\bar{u}$ is an inner automorphism (Chapter VIII, § 10, No. 1, Theorem 1). There therefore exists an element $s$ of $G$ such that $\varphi(s) = u$. On the other hand, the center of $C(Q)$ is contained in $C^+$, which entails that $\varphi(G)/\varphi(G^+)$ is isomorphic to $G/G^+$, hence that $\varphi(G^+)$ is of index 2 in $\varphi(G) = \mathbf{O}(Q)$ if $E \not= \{0\}$. This proves the first two assertions of c).

Suppose finally that $A$ is of characteristic $\not= 2$. Then every element $u$ of $\mathbf{O}(Q)$ is a product of symmetries with respect to hyperplanes orthogonal to nonsingular vectors $x_i (i = 1, \ldots, h)$ (\S 6, n° 4, prop. 5); consequently one has $u = (-1)^h \varphi(x_1 \ldots x_h)$ and $\det(u) = (-1)^h$. For $u$ to belong to $\mathbf{SO}(Q)$, it is necessary and sufficient that $h$ be pair, which shows that $\varphi(G^+) \supset \mathbf{SO}(Q)$. Since $\mathbf{SO}(Q)$ is of index 2 in $\mathbf{O}(Q)$ when $E \not= \{0\}$, one has $\varphi(G^+) = \mathbf{SO}(Q)$ if $E$ is of *even* dimension, which completes the proof of c). On the other hand, if the dimension of $E$ is *odd*, $\varphi(G)$ does not contain the orthogonal transformation $x \to -x$; indeed this extends to the principal automorphism $\alpha$ of $C(Q)$ (n° 1), and $\alpha$ is not an inner automorphism since the center $Z$ of $C(Q)$ contains a nonzero element of $C^-(Q)$ (th. 3). Thus $\varphi(G) \not= \mathbf{O}(Q)$, and, since $\varphi(G) \supset \varphi(G^+) \supset \mathbf{SO}(Q)$ and $\mathbf{SO}(Q)$ is of index 2 in $\mathbf{O}(Q)$, one has $\varphi(G) = \varphi(G^+) = \mathbf{SO}(Q)$. This proves d). Q.E.D.

The subgroup $\varphi(G^+)$ of $\mathbf{O}(Q)$, which is of index 2 if $E \not= \{0\}$, is called the *group of rotations* of $E$, and its elements are called *rotations*; it is denoted by $\mathbf{O}^+(Q)$. Notice that, if $A$ is not of characteristic 2, one has $\mathbf{O}^+(Q) = \mathbf{SO}(Q)$ (cf. exerc. 9).

#### Proposition 4 {#alg-ix-s9-prop-4 .statement}

*Let $\beta$ be the principal antiautomorphism of $C(Q)$ (n° 1). For every $s \in G^+$, $\beta(s)s$ is a scalar. The mapping $N : s \to \beta(s)s$ is a homomorphism of $G^+$ into the multiplicative group $A^*$ of nonzero elements of $A$.*

Indeed, for $s \in G^+$, one has $sEs^{-1} = E$, whence $\beta(s)^{-1}E\beta(s) = E$, which shows that $\beta(s) \in G^+$. Since $sx = (\varphi(s).x)s$ for all $x \in E$, one has $x\beta(s) = \beta(sx) = \beta(s)(\varphi(s).x)$, and consequently $\beta(s)sx = \beta(s)(\varphi(s).x)s = x\beta(s)s$, which implies that $\beta(s)s$ belongs to the center of $C(Q)$. Since, moreover, $\beta(s)s$ belongs to $C^+(Q)$, $\beta(s)s$ is a scalar (Theorem 2 and 3). Finally one has $\beta(st)st = \beta(t)\beta(s)st = \beta(s)s\beta(t)t$, that is to say $N(st) = N(s)N(t)$ for $s, t$ in $G^+$. QED.

The scalar $N(s) = \beta(s)s$ ($s \in G^+$) is called the spinor norm of $s$. We denote by $G_0^+$ and call the reduced Clifford group the kernel of the homomorphism $N$. The image $\varphi(G_0^+)$ is denoted by $O_0^+(Q)$ and is called the reduced orthogonal group of $Q$. Since the kernel of the restriction of $\varphi$ to $G^+$ is the set of even and invertible elements of the center of $C(Q)$ (Theorem 4) and is therefore identified with $A^*$ (Theorem 2 and 3), $\varphi(G^+)/O_0^+(Q)$ is isomorphic to $G^+/A^*G_0^+$, and hence also to $N(G^+)/N(A^*)$, and in particular commutative. It is clear that $N(A^*)$ is the subgroup $(A^*)^2$ of squares of elements of $A^*$. If $Q$ is of index $> 0$, there exist, whatever $a \in A^*$ may be, two elements $x$ and $y$ of $E$ such that $Q(x) = a$ and $Q(y) = 1$ (§ 4, No. 2, Proposition 4) ; since $xy \in G^+$ and $N(xy) = Q(x)Q(y) = a$, this shows that $N(G^+) = A^*$, and hence that $\varphi(G^+)/O_0^+(Q)$ is isomorphic to $A^*/(A^*)^2$.
