## § 7. Hermitian forms and ordered fields

Throughout this paragraph, let K denote a maximal ordered field (hence commutative and of characteristic zero; cf. chap. VI, § 2), and suppose that we are in one of the following three cases:

1°) A = K, J is the identity;
2°) A is the field K(i), obtained by adjunction to K of a square root i of −1, and, for every λ ∈ A, $\overline{\lambda}$ is the conjugate of λ (chap. II, § 7, no 7).
3°) A is the quaternion field over K corresponding to the pair (−1, −1) (or, as we shall say for brevity, the quaternion field over K), and, for every λ ∈ A, $\overline{\lambda}$ is the conjugate of λ (cf. chap. II, § 7, no 8 and chap. VIII, § 11, no 2).

If Φ is a hermitian form on E, we therefore have, in all cases, $\Phi(x, x) \in K$ for every $x \in E$, since $\Phi(x, x) = \overline{\Phi(x, x)}$.

### 1. Positive hermitian forms.

#### Definition 1 {#alg-ix-s7-def-1 .statement}

A hermitian form $\Phi$ on $E$ is said to be positive (resp. negative) if $\Phi(x, x) \geqslant 0$ (resp. $\Phi(x, x) \leqslant 0$) for every $x \in E$.

When $A = K$, one also says that the quadratic form
$$
Q(x) = \frac{1}{2} \Phi(x, x)
$$
associated with $\Phi$ is positive (resp. negative).

Suppose $E$ is finite-dimensional over $A$, and let $(e_i)$ ($i = 1, \ldots, n$) be an orthogonal basis of $E$ ($\S 6$, no 1, th. 1). For a hermitian form $\Phi$ on $E$ to be positive, it is necessary and sufficient that $\Phi(e_i, e_i) \geqslant 0$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$. Let $\Phi$ be a positive non-degenerate hermitian form on $E$; since every positive element of $K$ is a square, hence of the form $\rho \overline{\rho}$ ($\rho \in A$), there exist orthonormal bases for $\Phi$ in $E$ ($\S 6$, no 1, cor. 1 of th. 1).

#### Proposition 1 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-1 .statement}

Suppose $E$ is finite-dimensional, and $A = K$ or $A = K(i)$. If $\Phi$ is a positive non-degenerate hermitian form on $E$, then the extensions of $\Phi$ to $\bigotimes^p E$ and $\wedge^p E$ ($p > 0$), as well as the inverse form of $\Phi$, are positive non-degenerate hermitian forms.

This follows at once from the existence of an orthonormal basis of $E$, and from prop. 2, $\S 6$, no 1.

Prop. 1 remains true if « positive non-degenerate » is replaced everywhere by « positive ».

#### Proposition 2 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-2 .statement}

Let $\Phi$ be a positive hermitian form on $E$. For $x, y$ in $E$, one has
$$
\Phi(x, y) \overline{\Phi(x, y)} \leqslant \Phi(x, x) \Phi(y, y).
$$

The inequality is indeed immediate when the vectors $x$ and $y$ are proportional. We may therefore suppose that $x$ and $y$ are linearly independent. Let $A'$ be the (commutative) subfield $K(\Phi(x, y))$ of $A$, $F$ the vector plane over $A'$ generated by $x$ and $y$, and $\Phi_F$ the restriction of $\Phi$ to $F$; the latter takes its values in $A'$. By Proposition 1, the discriminant of $\Phi_F$ with respect to the basis $(x, y)$ is $\geqslant 0$. Now this discriminant is $\Phi(x, x)\Phi(y, y) - \Phi(x, y)\overline{\Phi(x, y)}$. Q.E.D.

#### Corollary {#alg-ix-s7-n1-cor-1 .statement}

*The set of isotropic vectors of E is the orthogonal subspace $E^0$ of E for $\Phi$. In order that $\Phi$ be non-degenerate, it is necessary and sufficient that $\Phi(x, x) > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$.

#### Proposition 3 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-3 .statement}

*Suppose E finite-dimensional and A commutative ($\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}$ or $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}(i)$). Let $\Phi$ be a hermitian form on E, X its matrix with respect to a basis $(x_j)$ ($j = 1, \ldots, n$) of E; for every subset H of $\{1, n\}$, denote by $X_{H, H}$ the minor of X obtained by deleting the rows and columns with indices $j \notin H$ (Chap. III, § 6, No. 3).

a) *If $\Phi$ is positive non-degenerate, then $X_{H, H} > 0$ for every subset H of $\{1, n\}$.

b) *Conversely, if, on putting $H_j = \{1, j\}$, we have $X_{H_j, H_j} > 0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, n$, then $\Phi$ is positive non-degenerate.

Suppose first that $\Phi$ is positive non-degenerate. The elements $(x_j)$ ($j \in H$) form a basis of a subspace F of E, and the minor $X_{H, H}$ is the discriminant of the restriction $\Phi_F$ of $\Phi$ to F with respect to this basis; now, since $\Phi_F(x, x) > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$ in F, $\Phi_F$ is positive non-degenerate (Corollary to Proposition 2); hence $X_{H, H} > 0$ (Proposition 1). To prove b), let us note that, with the notation of Proposition 1, § 6, No. 1, the minor $X_{H_j, H_j}$ is equal to $D_{j+1, j+1}$; there therefore exists (Proposition 1, § 6, No. 1) an orthogonal basis $(e_j)$ ($j = 1, \ldots, n$) of E such that $\Phi(e_j, e_j) > 0$ for $j = 1, \ldots, n$; consequently $\Phi$ is positive non-degenerate.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s7-n1-rem-1 .statement}

It follows from the existence of orthonormal bases that two non-degenerate positive hermitian forms on two vector spaces of the same finite dimension are *equivalent* (§ 1, no 6). Let then L be a finite-dimensional hermitian space over A, whose metric form is positive non-degenerate, and let $V_1$ and $V_2$ be two linear varieties of *the same dimension* in L (§ 6, no 6); since the restrictions of the metric form to the directions $T_1$ and $T_2$ of $V_1$ and $V_2$ on the one hand, and to the orthogonal subspaces $T_1^0$ and $T_2^0$ on the other hand, are equivalent, there exists a unitary automorphism $u$ of the space $T$ of the translations of $L$ such that $u(T_1) = T_2$ and $u(T_1^0) = T_2^0$; there therefore exists a displacement $v$ of $L$ such that $v(V_1) = V_2$. Let $(a, b), (a', b')$ be two pairs of points of $L$; for there to exist a displacement $v$ of $L$ such that $v(a) = a'$ and $v(b) = b'$, it is therefore necessary and sufficient (with the notation of § 6, no 6) that one has $e(a, b) = e(a', b')$; the element $\sqrt{e(a, b)}$ of $A$ (chap. VI, § 2, no 4) is called the distance between $a$ and $b$ in the hermitian space $L$.

### 2. The law of inertia.

#### Theorem 1 (« law of inertia ») {#alg-ix-s7-thm-1 .statement}

Suppose that $A$ satisfies the hypotheses at the beginning of this paragraph, and that $E$ is of finite dimension $n$. Let $\Phi$ be a hermitian form on $E$. Then:

a) There exists a decomposition of $E$ into a direct sum of the subspace $E^0$ orthogonal to $E$, and of two subspaces $E^+$ and $E^-$ such that the restriction of $\Phi$ to $E^+$ (resp. $E^-$) is positive (resp. negative) and non-degenerate.

b) There exists an orthogonal basis $(e_i)_{1 \leq i \leq n}$ of $E$ such that

$$
\Phi\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i e_i, \sum_{i=1}^n \eta_i e_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^s \xi_i \overline{\eta_i} - \sum_{i=s+1}^{s+t} \xi_i \overline{\eta_i}.
$$

c) The dimensions $s$ of $E^+$ and $t$ of $E^-$ are the same for all the decompositions into a direct sum satisfying the conditions stated in a) ; the integer $s$ (resp. $t$) is the maximum of the dimensions of the subspaces $F$ of $E$ such that the restriction of $\Phi$ to $F$ is positive (resp. negative) and non-degenerate.

d) The rank of $\Phi$ is $s + t$.

e) If $\Phi$ is non-degenerate, its index is equal to $\inf(s, t)$ (\S 4, no 2, def. 2).

Indeed, let $(x_i)$ ($i = 1, \ldots, n$) be an orthogonal basis of $E$ (\S 6, No. 1, Theorem 1); recall that $\Phi(x, x) \in K$ for every $x \in E$. We may suppose that $\Phi(x_i, x_i) > 0$ for $i = 1, \ldots, s$, $\Phi(x_i, x_i) < 0$ for $i = s + 1, \ldots, s + t$ and $\Phi(x_i, x_i) = 0$ for $i = s + t + 1, \ldots, n$. This proves a), since we take for $E^+$ (resp. $E^-$) the subspace generated by $x_1, \ldots, x_s$ (resp. by $x_{s+1}, \ldots, x_{s+t}$), and $E^0$ is then generated by $x_{s+t+1}, \ldots, x_n$. We deduce b) from this by observing that $\Phi(x_i, x_i)$ is of the form $\rho \overline{\rho}$ (resp.

- $\rho \bar{\rho} )\ (\rho \in A^*)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, s$ (resp. $i = s + 1, \ldots, s + t$). To prove c), consider a subspace P of E such that the restriction of $\Phi$ to P is positive and non-degenerate; we then have $P \cap (E^- + E^0) = \{0\}$, and the sum $P + E^- + E^0$ is therefore direct; we conclude that $\dim P \leq \dim E^+ = s$, and this proves c). Assertion d) follows at once from a).

Suppose finally that $\Phi$ is non-degenerate, put $q = \inf (s, t)$, and show that q is the index of $\Phi$. With the notations of b), the vectors $e_i + e_{s+i}$ (resp. $e_i - e_{s+i}$) ($i = 1, \ldots, q$) generate a totally isotropic subspace F (resp. $F'$). Since K is of characteristic 0, $F + F'$ is generated by $e_1, \ldots, e_q, e_{s+1}, \ldots, e_{s+q}$ and is therefore non-isotropic. The restriction of $\Phi$ to the subspace $H = (F + F')^0$ is then positive (or negative) and non-degenerate, and H contains therefore no isotropic vector $\neq 0$. Since $F^0$ contains F and H, and since $\dim(F + H) = \operatorname{codim} F' = \operatorname{codim} F$, we have $F^0 = F + H$. Thus an isotropic vector z orthogonal to F is necessarily in F, because, in the direct sum $F + H$, the component of z in H is isotropic. Consequently F is a maximal totally isotropic subspace, and this proves e).

#### Definition 2 {#alg-ix-s7-def-2 .statement}

With the notations of Theorem 1, the pair $(s, t)$ is called the signature of $\Phi$.

### 3. Reduction of a form with respect to a positive hermitian form.

In this No. we shall suppose that E is of finite dimension $n$ over $A$, and we shall denote by $\Phi$ a non-degenerate positive hermitian form on E.

Since the linear mappings of E into $E^*$ associated with $\Phi$ are bijective, whatever the elements $x, y, z$ of E with $y \neq 0$, there exists one and only one element $t$ of E such that $\Phi(x, z) = \overline{\Phi(t, y)}$. In particular, if $A = K$ or $A = K(i)$ and if $u$ is a semilinear mapping for J of E into itself (chap. II, App. I, No. 1), there exists, for every $x \in E$, one and only one element $u^*(x)$ such that for every $y \in E$, $\Phi(x, u(y)) = \overline{\Phi(u^*(x), y)}$. It is immediately seen that $u^*$ is a semilinear mapping for J of E into itself; it is called the adjoint of $u$.

#### Remark 1 {#alg-ix-s7-n3-rem-1 .statement}

When J is the identity, we recover the notion of adjoint of a homomorphism defined in § 1, No. 8.

#### Remark 2 {#alg-ix-s7-n3-rem-2 .statement}

Suppose still that $A = K$ or $A = K(i)$. Let $E^J$ be the right A-module defined in § 1, No. 2, Def. 5. The form $\Phi$ is a bilinear form on $E \times E^J$, $\Phi^J$ is a bilinear form on $E^J \times E$, and $u$ is an A-linear mapping of E into $E^J$. The adjoint of this homomorphism, in the sense of § 1, No. 8, is an A-linear mapping of E into $E^J$; it is immediately seen that this coincides with the mapping $u^*$ defined above.

An endomorphism $u$ of E is said to be normal (for $\Phi$) if $uu^* = u^*u$.

Examples of normal endomorphisms:
1) the unitary automorphisms for $\Phi$ ($\S$ 6, No. 2), which are characterized by the relation $u^{-1} = u^*$ ($\S$ 1, No. 8, cor. of prop. 8);
2) the endomorphisms $u$ such that $u^* = u$; these are called hermitian endomorphisms.

For every hermitian endomorphism $u$ of E, put $\Phi_u(x, y) = \Phi(u(x), y)$; we have
$$
\Phi_u(y, x) = \Phi(u(y), x) = \Phi(y, u(x)) = \overline{\Phi(u(x), y)} = \overline{\Phi_u(x, y)}.
$$
which shows that $\Phi_u$ is a hermitian form on E. Conversely let $\Psi$ be a hermitian form on E; since the mapping $s_\Phi$ of E into $E^*$ associated with $\Phi$ is bijective, there exists, for every $x \in E$, one and only one element $u(x)$ of E such that $\Psi(x, y) = \Phi(u(x), y)$; it is easily verified that $u$ is a hermitian endomorphism of E. Thus $u \to \Phi_u$ is a canonical bijection of the set of hermitian endomorphisms of E onto the set of hermitian forms on E.

Suppose that $A = K$ or $A = K(i)$; given a bilinear form $\Psi$ on E, there exists, for every $x \in E$, one and only one element $u(x)$ of E such that $\Psi(x, y) = \overline{\Phi(u(x), y)}$; it is immediately seen that $u$ is a semilinear mapping (for J) of E into itself. Since $\Phi(u(x), y) = \Phi(y, u(x)) = \overline{\Phi(u^*(y), x)}$, we see that, for $\Psi$ to be symmetric (resp. alternating), it is necessary and sufficient that $u^* = u$ (resp. $u^* = -u$).

#### Theorem 2 {#alg-ix-s7-thm-2 .statement}

Let S be a set of endomorphisms of E (resp. of semilinear mappings of E into E when A = K or A = K(i)) stable under the mapping u → u*. Then, if V is a subspace of E stable under S, its orthogonal V⁰ is stable under S. On the other hand E is the direct sum of subspaces stable under S, minimal in the set of subspaces ≠ {0} and stable under S, and pairwise orthogonal.

Indeed let V be a subspace of E stable under S; whatever x ∈ V⁰, y ∈ V and u ∈ S, we have u*(y) ∈ V, whence $\Phi(y, u(x)) = \Phi(u*(y), x) = 0$ (resp. $\Phi(y, u(x)) = \overline{\Phi(u^*(y), x)} = 0$), and consequently u(x) ∈ V⁰; thus V⁰ is stable under S, which proves our first assertion. For the second we shall proceed by induction on the dimension n of E, the case n = 0 being trivial. For n ≠ 0 there exists a subspace V ≠ {0} of E stable under S and minimal, for example a stable subspace ≠ {0} of minimal dimension. It then suffices to apply the induction hypothesis to V⁰, since the adjoint of the restriction of u to V⁰ (with respect to the restriction of $\Phi$) is identical with the restriction to V⁰ of the adjoint of u.

#### Corollary 1 {#alg-ix-s7-thm-2-cor-1 .statement}

Suppose that A = K or that A = K(i). Let B be a subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}_A(E)$, stable under the mapping u → u*. Then E is a semisimple B-module, and is the direct sum of simple submodules pairwise orthogonal. The algebra B is semisimple.

Indeed, since every B-submodule V of E has a supplement, for example V⁰, the B-module V is semisimple (chap. VIII, § 3, No. 3, prop. 7). Since every B-submodule ≠ {0} and minimal in E is simple, E is the direct sum of simple submodules pairwise orthogonal. Finally B is a semisimple algebra, since it admits a semisimple and faithful module E whose contramodule is of finite type (chap. VIII, § 5, No. 1, prop. 3).

#### Corollary 2 {#alg-ix-s7-thm-2-cor-2 .statement}

The assumptions and notations being those of cor. 1, suppose in addition that the algebra B is commutative. Then all its elements are semisimple (normal) endomorphisms of E. When A = K (resp. A = K(i)), E is the direct sum of simple B-submodules pairwise orthogonal, which are vector spaces of dimension 1 or 2 (resp. 1) over A.

The first assertion follows from chap. VIII, § 9, No. 1, prop. 2, since B is semisimple. On the other hand every simple B-module is isomorphic to a B-module of the form B/m, where m is a maximal ideal of B, hence to a commutative field L of finite degree over A; when A = K (resp. A = K(i)), the field L is necessarily isomorphic to K or K(i) (resp. K(i)), since K(i) is algebraically closed (chap. VI, § 2, No. 6, th. 3).

#### Proposition 4 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-4 .statement}

Let u be a normal endomorphism of E. When A is equal to K(i) or to the quaternion field over K, there exists an orthonormal basis (for Φ) of E formed by eigenvectors of u. When A = K, u is semisimple and E is direct sum of stable subspaces for u, pairwise orthogonal, and of dimension 1 or 2.

First examine the case where A is commutative (A = K or A = K(i)). Then the subalgebra B = A[u, u*] of $\mathcal{L}_A(E)$ is commutative since u is normal; it is stable under the mapping $\varphi \to \varphi^*$ by virtue of formulas (32) and (33) of § 1, No. 8. The assertion concerning the case A = K then follows immediately from cor. 2 of th. 2. When A = K(i), this corollary also shows that E is direct sum of vector subspaces $Ax_i$ ($i = 1, \ldots, n$) of dimension 1, pairwise orthogonal and stable for u; if one puts $e_i = (\Phi(x_i, x_i))^{-1/2} x_i$, $(e_i)$ is the orthonormal basis sought.

When A is the quaternion field over K, it will suffice similarly to prove, by virtue of th. 2, that every minimal element of the set of subspaces $\neq \{0\}$ of E stable for u and $u^*$ is of dimension 1. Now such a subspace V necessarily contains an eigenvector $x \neq 0$ of u (*), as is seen by observing that the quaternion field A contains K(i) as an algebraically closed subfield, and by restricting to K(i) the field of

(*) If E is a left vector space over a noncommutative field A, and u an endomorphism of E, one still says that a vector $x \neq 0$ of E is an eigenvector for u if there exists $a \in A$ such that $u(x) = ax$; the scalar a is then called an eigenvalue of u. One will note that, for every $b \neq 0$ in A, the vector $bx$ is an eigenvector for u, and that the corresponding eigenvalue is $bab^{-1}$.

scalars of V. It remains therefore only to show that the eigenvector x of u is also an eigenvector of u*. Put $u(x) = ax$ ($a \in A$); one then has $\Phi(u(x), x) = a\Phi(x, x) = \Phi(x, x)a = \Phi(x, \overline{a}x)$ since $\Phi(x, x)$ belongs to the center of A, and on the other hand $\Phi(u(x), x) = \Phi(x, u^*(x))$; it follows that one has $\Phi(x, u^*(x) - \overline{a}x) = 0$, and one can therefore write $u^*(x) = \overline{a}x + z$, where z is a vector orthogonal to x. One therefore has

$$
\Phi(u^*(x), u^*(x)) = \overline{a}a\Phi(x, x) + \Phi(z, z) = \Phi(u(x), u(x)) + \Phi(z, z).
$$

Now, since u is normal, one has

$$
\begin{align*}
\Phi(u(x), u(x)) &= \Phi(x, u^*u(x)) = \Phi(x, uu^*(x)) \\
&= \Phi(uu^*(x), x) = \Phi(u^*(x), u^*(x)).
\end{align*}

Consequently one has $\Phi(z, z) = 0$, which, by assumption, entails $z = 0$ and $u^*(x) = \overline{a}x$. QED.

#### Remark {#alg-ix-s7-n3-rem-28 .statement}

It follows from th. 2 that the eigenspaces relative to two distinct eigenvalues of u are orthogonal.

#### Proposition 5 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-5 .statement}

Let u be a hermitian endomorphism of E. The eigenvalues of u belong to K, and there exists an orthonormal basis of E formed of eigenvectors of u.

When A is equal to K(i) or to the quaternion field over K, it suffices, by virtue of prop. 4, to prove the first assertion; now, if x is an eigenvector $\neq 0$ of u and a the corresponding eigenvalue, one has, by virtue of the assumption $u = u^*$,

$$
a\Phi(x, x) = \Phi(u(x), x) = \Phi(x, u(x)) = \Phi(x, x)\overline{a} ;
$$

as $\Phi(x, x)$ is a nonzero element of the center of A, it follows that $a = \overline{a}$, hence $a \in K$. Consequently a hermitian matrix has all its eigenvalues in K. Suppose now that A is equal to K. The matrix M of u with respect to an orthonormal basis of E is then symmetric; it is therefore a hermitian matrix if one considers it as a matrix over K(i). The first part of the proof then shows that this matrix has all its eigenvalues in K, and admits therefore, if $E \neq \{0\}$, nonzero eigenvectors in E. It follows that every stable subspace for u and minimal is necessarily of dimension 1, and the conclusion follows immediately, as in prop. 4.

#### Proposition 6 {#alg-ix-s7-prop-6 .statement}

a) Let $\Psi$ be a hermitian form (resp. symmetric bilinear form if $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}$ or $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}(i)$) on $E$. There exists a basis of $E$ which is orthonormal for $\Phi$ and orthogonal for $\Psi$.

b) Suppose $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}$ or $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{K}(i)$, and let $\Psi$ be an alternating bilinear form on $E$. There exists a basis of $E$ which is orthonormal for $\Phi$ and with respect to which the matrix of $\Psi$ is of the form

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & a_1 & 0 & 0 \ldots 0 \\
-a_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \ldots 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & a_2 \ldots 0 \\
0 & 0 & -a_2 & 0 \ldots 0 \\
\cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

where the $a_i$ are $\geqslant 0$ in K.

When $\Psi$ is hermitian, our assertion follows at once from Prop. 5 and the canonical correspondence between hermitian forms on $E$ and hermitian endomorphisms for $\Phi$. In the two other cases, let $u$ be the semilinear mapping of $E$ into itself defined at the beginning of this No. by the formula $\Psi(x, y) = \overline{\Phi(u(x), y)}$. Then $u^2$ is an $\mathbf{A}$-linear mapping; when $\Psi$ is symmetric (resp. alternating), one has $u = u^*$ (resp. $u = -u^*$), hence $u^2$ is hermitian; whence also

$$
\Phi(u^2(x), x) = \overline{\Phi(x, u^2(x))} = \Phi(u^*(x), u(x)) = \Phi(u(x), u(x))
$$
(resp. $-\Phi(u(x), u(x))$);

this shows that the hermitian form $(x, y) \to \Phi(u^2(x), y)$ is positive (resp. negative). Apply then Theorem 2, and let $V$ be a minimal element of the family of the subspaces $\neq \{0\}$ of $E$ stable for $u$ and for $u^*$. Since $u^2$ is a hermitian endomorphism, $V$ contains a proper vector $x \neq 0$ of $u^2$; put $u^2(x) = ax$, where $a \in \mathbf{K}$ (Prop. 5).

When $\Psi$ is symmetric, the inequality $\Phi(u^2(x), x) \geqslant 0$ shows that one has $a \geqslant 0$. Put $y = a^{1/2}x + u(x)$; one has $u(y) = a^{1/2}u(x) + ax = a^{1/2}y$, and $Ay$ is stable for $u$ and $u^* = u$. If $y = 0$, $Ax$ is stable for $u$ and $u^*$; in all cases, $V$ is a subspace of dimension 1, and our conclusion follows at once from Theorem 2.

When $\Psi$ is alternating, one has $a \leqslant 0$; put

$$
y = (-a)^{1/2}x + u(x), \quad z = (-a)^{1/2}x - u(x).
$$

One has $u(y) = -(-a)^{1/2}z, \ u(z) = (-a)^{1/2}y$ and
$$
\Phi(y, z) = -a\Phi(x, x) - \Phi(u(x), u(x)) = -\Phi(ax, x) + \Phi(u^2(x), x) = 0.
$$

If $y = z = 0$, we have $a = 0$ and $u(x) = 0$, hence $Ax$ is stable under $u$ and $u^*$, $V$ is of dimension 1, and the matrix of the restriction of $\Psi$ to $V$ is zero since $\Psi$ is alternating. Otherwise, $y$ and $z$ are both $\neq 0$, they generate $V$, and since they are orthogonal, $V$ is of dimension 2 and the matrix of the restriction of $\Psi$ to $V$ is of the form $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & b \\ -b & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Finally, by virtue of the formula $\Psi'(x', y') = \overline{\Phi(u(x'), y')}$, we have $\Psi'(x', y') = 0$ when $x'$ and $y'$ belong to two subspaces stable under $u$ and orthogonal for $\Phi$. This proves the existence of an orthonormal basis of $E$ (for $\Phi$) with respect to which the matrix of $\Psi$ has the indicated form. Q.E.D.

Exercises. — 1) Suppose that the hypotheses at the beginning of § 7 are satisfied; let $\Phi$ be a positive Hermitian form on $E$.

a) In order that $\Phi(x, x)\Phi(y, y) = \Phi(x, y)\overline{\Phi(x, y)}$, it is necessary and sufficient that $x$ and $y$ be linearly dependent or that the plane generated by $x$ and $y$ be isotropic.

#### Remark 27 {#alg-ix-s7-n3-rem-27 .statement}

Let K be a maximal ordered field, E a vector space of dimension n over K, $\Phi$ a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on E, of signature (s, t) distinct from (n, 0) and from (0, n). Let $(e_i)$ be an orthogonal basis for $\Phi$, such that $\Phi(e_i, e_i) = 1$ for $1 \leq i \leq s$, $\Phi(e_i, e_i) = -1$ for $s+1 \leq i \leq n$. For every orthogonal transform $u \in \mathbf{U}(\Phi)$, let

$$
U = \begin{pmatrix} M & N \\ P & Q \end{pmatrix}
$$

be the matrix of u with respect to $(e_i)$, written in the form of a square tableau of matrices corresponding to the partition of $(1, n)$ into $(1, s)$ and $(s+1, n)$.

a) Prove the relations

$$
\begin{align*}
{}^tM \cdot M - {}^tP \cdot P &= I_s \\
{}^tQ \cdot Q - {}^tN \cdot N &= I_t \\
{}^tM \cdot N - {}^tP \cdot Q &= 0.
\end{align*}
$$

b) Let R be a matrix over K with t rows and s columns, such that $I_s - {}^tR \cdot R$ is the matrix of a positive non-degenerate hermitian form (on $K^s$). Show that $\det(M + \lambda NR)$ does not change sign for $-1 \leq \lambda \leq 1$ in K (show, using a), that ${}^t(M + \lambda NR)(M + \lambda NR)$ is the matrix of a positive non-degenerate symmetric form).

c) Put $\sigma(u) = \sigma(U) = \operatorname{sgn}(\det M)$; show that, for any two elements $u, v$ of the orthogonal group $\mathbf{U}(\Phi)$, one has $\sigma(uv) = \sigma(u)\sigma(v)$ (use a) and b)). Deduce that the commutator group of the special orthogonal group $\mathbf{SU}(\Phi)$ is distinct from $\mathbf{SU}(\Phi)$ (cf. § 10, exerc. 9).
