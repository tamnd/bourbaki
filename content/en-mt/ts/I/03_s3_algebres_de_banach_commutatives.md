## § 3. COMMUTATIVE BANACH ALGEBRAS

In this section, the base field is $\mathbf{C}$.

### 1. Characters of a commutative Banach algebra

#### Theorem 1 {#ts-i-s3-thm-1 .statement tag=027P}

Let A be a Banach algebra and let $\chi : A\rightarrow \mathbf{C}$ be an algebra morphism (cf. I, p. 9). Then $\chi$ is continuous, of norm at most 1. If A is unital and if $\chi$ is a unital morphism, then $\chi$ is of norm 1.

Let us prove that $|\chi (x)|\leqslant \|x\|$ for every $x\in$ A. Replacing A, if necessary, by the Banach algebra generated by $x$, we may suppose that A is commutative; then, $\chi \in \mathsf{X}'(A)$. For every $x\in A$, one has $\chi (x)\in$ Sp$'_A(x)$ (I, p. 9, No.$^o7$), hence $|\chi (x)|\leqslant \varrho (x)\leqslant \|x\|($I, p. 28, cor. 5), whence the first assertion.

If, moreover, A is unital, the equality $\chi (1) = 1$ implies that $\|\chi \|\geqslant 1$, whence the required equality.

#### Remark {#ts-i-s3-n1-rem-1 .statement tag=027Q}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. It follows from this theorem that the topology of simple convergence on $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ coincides with the restriction to $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ of the weak topology $\sigma (A',A)$ of the dual $A'$ of A.

#### Corollary {#ts-i-s3-n1-cor-1 .statement tag=027R}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. The space $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is compact. The space $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is locally compact, and is compact if A admits a unit element.

Let $A'$ be the dual of the Banach space A. Its unit ball $A'_1$ is weakly compact (EVT, III, p. 17, cor. 2). By Theorem 1, one has $\mathsf{X}'(A)\subset A'_1$. Moreover, $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is closed in $A'$ for the weak topology, because it is the intersection of the weakly closed sets

$X_{x,y}=\{f\in A'|f(xy)-f(x)f(y) = 0\}$ (for $x,y\in A$).

It follows that $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is compact and that $\mathsf{X}(A) =\mathsf{X}'(A)-\{0\}$ is locally compact.

If A admits a unit element 1, then $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is the set of $\chi \in$ $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ such that $\chi (1) = 1$, and is therefore a closed subset, and hence compact, of $\mathsf{X}'(A)$.

Every compact space is homeomorphic to $\mathsf{X}(A)$ for a suitable commutative unital Banach algebra A (cf. I, p. 32, cor. 2).

#### Theorem 2 {#ts-i-s3-thm-2 .statement tag=027S}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. The mapping $\chi \mapsto$ Ker($\chi$ ) is a bijection of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ onto the set J(A) of regular maximal ideals of A.

Let I be a regular maximal ideal of A. It is closed (I, p. 22, cor. 2), hence $A/I$ is a Banach algebra. Since it is a field (A, VIII, p. 426, prop. 2), the Gelfand-Mazur theorem (I, p. 26, cor. 2) implies that $A/I$ is of dimension 1 over $\mathbf{C}$. Hence I is of codimension 1 in A. The theorem then follows from Lemma 3 of I, p. 10.

It may happen that a nonzero commutative Banach algebra A, without a unit element, has no maximal ideal; then $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is reduced to $\{0\}($cf. I, p. 186, exercise 31).

This theorem shows that the sets J(A) and $\widehat{A}$ of I, p. 11, which are identified since A is commutative, may also be identified with the set $\mathsf{X}(A)$. There is therefore reason to consider on $\mathsf{X}(A)$ the weak topology, and the Jacobson topology which is less fine (I, p. 15, prop. 5). The closed sets V(M) of the Jacobson topology, for $M\subset A$, are the sets

$$
\{\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)|\chi (M) = 0\}
$$

of $\mathsf{X}(A)$, which will sometimes still be denoted by V(M). Similarly, for every subset M of $\mathsf{X}(A)$, we shall denote by Υ(M) the ideal intersection of the kernels of the $\chi \in M$; it is equal to the set $\Upsilon (M')$ defined in I, p. 13 for the subset $M'\subset J(A)$ corresponding to M when J(A) and $\mathsf{X}(A)$ are identified.

When a topological notion is used in $\mathsf{X}(A)$ without specifying which topology is meant, it will always be the weak topology.

### 2. Continuous functions vanishing at infinity on a locally compact space

In this number, X is a locally compact space. One denotes by $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ the commutative Banach algebra of complex-valued continuous functions tending to 0 at infinity on X, endowed with the norm $\|f\|=$ sup$_{x\in X}|f(x)|$

(Example 3 of I, p. 17).

#### Proposition 1 {#ts-i-s3-prop-1 .statement tag=027T}

For every closed subset Φ of X, let $I_{\Phi}$ be the set of $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ that are zero on Φ. Then $\Phi \mapsto I_{\Phi}$ is a bijection of the set of closed subsets of X onto the set of closed ideals of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$.

The set $I_{\Phi}$ is a closed ideal of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$.

Let $\Phi \not= \Phi '$ be closed subsets of X. Exchanging Φ and $\Phi '$ if necessary, we may suppose that there exists $x\in \Phi '$ such that $x\notin \Phi$, and then there exists a function $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ which is zero on Φ and non-zero at $x$ (TG, IX, p. 43, prop. 1). We have $f\in I_{\Phi}$ and $f\notin I_{\Phi'}$, so that the mapping $\Phi \mapsto I_{\Phi}$ is injective.

Let I be a closed ideal of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$. Let Φ be the set of $x\in X$ such that $f(x) = 0$ for every $f\in I$; this is a closed subset of X, and one has $I\subset I_{\Phi}$. Let us prove that $I_{\Phi}\subset I$, which will imply that $I = I_{\Phi}$ and complete the proof of the proposition.

Let $f\in I_{\Phi}$. For every real number $\varepsilon  >0$, let $C_{\varepsilon}$ denote the set of $x\in X$ such that $|f(x)|\geqslant \varepsilon$. Since $f$ tends to 0 at infinity, the set $C_{\varepsilon}$ is compact. The open sets $V_x\cap C_{\varepsilon}$ cover $C_{\varepsilon}$. Since the set $C_{\varepsilon}$ is compact, there exists a finite subset $T_{\varepsilon}\subset X$ such that

$$
C_{\varepsilon}\subset \bigcup_{x\in T_{\varepsilon}}V_x
$$

#### Corollary 1 {#ts-i-s3-prop-1-cor-1 .statement tag=027U}

For every $x\in X$, let $I_x$ be the set of $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ vanishing at $x$. Then $x\mapsto I_x$ is a bijection of X onto the set of maximal closed ideals of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$. These ideals are regular.

This follows at once from Prop. 1.

Let us denote by $X'$ the Alexandroff compactification of X, that is, the compact space deduced from X by adjunction of a point at infinity $\omega_X$ (TG, I, p. 67 and 68). The algebra $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ is identified with the Banach algebra of continuous complex-valued functions on $X'$ vanishing at $\omega_X$.

For every $x\in X'$, we denote by ev$_x$ the character of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ defined by ev$_x(f) =f(x)$ for every $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(X)$.

#### Corollary 2 {#ts-i-s3-prop-1-cor-2 .statement tag=027V}

The mapping $x\mapsto$ ev$_x$ is a homeomorphism of $X'$ onto $\mathsf{X}'(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$, and its restriction to X is a homeomorphism of X onto $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$. Moreover, the weak topology and the Jacobson topology coincide on $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$.

The mapping ev$:x\mapsto$ ev$_x$ of $X'$ into $\mathsf{X}'(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$ is injective. It is surjective by Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 of I, p. 30. It is continuous, for for every function $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ and every open subset U of $\mathbf{R}$, we have

ev$^{-1}(\{\chi \in \mathsf{X}'(\mathscr{C}_0(X))|\chi (f)\in U\}) =\overset{-1}{f}(U)$

which is open in X. The mapping ev is therefore a homeomorphism since $X'$ is compact. The restriction of ev to X is then a homeomorphism onto $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$.

If F is a weakly closed subset of $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$, it corresponds under the homeomorphism ev to a closed subset Φ of X; precisely, by Prop. 1, we have $F =\{\chi \in \mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))|I_{\Phi}\subset$ Ker $\chi \}$, which is closed for the Jacobson topology.

#### Corollary 3 {#ts-i-s3-prop-1-cor-3 .statement tag=027W}

Suppose X compact. Then the mapping $x\mapsto$ ev$_x$ is a homeomorphism of X onto $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}(X))$. The weak topology and the Jacobson topology coincide on $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}(X))$.

### 3. Proper partial mappings

In this No., X and Y are locally compact topological spaces. We denote by $X'$ (resp. $Y'$) the compact space obtained from X (resp. Y) by adjunction of a point at infinity $\omega_X$ (resp. $\omega_Y$) (TG, I, p. 67–68). We identify $X'$ and $Y'$ with $\mathsf{X}'(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$ and $\mathsf{X}'(\mathscr{C}_0(Y))$, respectively (Corollary 2 of I, p. 32).

#### Definition 1 {#ts-i-s3-def-1 .statement tag=027X}

A proper partial mapping from X into Y is a correspondence $f= (\Gamma ,X,Y)$ (E, II, p. 10, Def. 2) between X and Y such that

(i) The graph Γ is functional;

(ii) The domain of definition of $f$ is an open subset U of X;

(iii) The mapping $x\mapsto f(x)$ of U into Y is proper.

The identity mapping of X is a proper partial mapping of X into X. Let Z be a locally compact topological space and $f$ (resp. $g$) a proper partial mapping of X into Y (resp. of Y into Z). Then the composite correspondence $g\circ f$ (E, II, p. 11, Def. 6) is a proper partial mapping of X into Z (TG, I, p. 72, Prop. 3, and p. 73, Prop. 5).

#### Lemma 1 {#ts-i-s3-lem-1 .statement tag=027Y}

For every proper partial mapping $f$ of X into Y, with domain of definition U, let us denote by $\widetilde{f}$ the mapping of $X'$ into $Y'$ defined by $\widetilde{f}(x) =f(x)$ if $x\in U$ and $\widetilde{f}(x) =\omega_Y$ if $x \notin U$; it is continuous.

The mapping $f\mapsto \widetilde{f}$ is a bijection between the set of proper partial mappings $f$ of X into Y and the set of continuous mappings $g$ of $X'$ into $Y'$ such that $g(\omega_X) =\omega_Y$.

Let $f$ be a proper partial mapping of X into Y, and let U be its domain. We shall prove that the mapping $\widetilde{f}$ is continuous. It is continuous at every point of U, since U is open in $X'$. We shall prove that it is also continuous at every point $x$ of $X'-$ U; then $\widetilde{f}(x) =\omega_Y$. Let V be an open neighbourhood of $\omega_Y$ in $Y'$; we shall prove that $\widetilde{f}^{-1}(V)$ is a neighbourhood of $x$. By definition of the topological space $Y'$, we may suppose that V is of the form $Y'-$ K, where K is a compact subset of Y. Since $f$ defines a proper mapping of U into Y, the set $f^{-1}(K)$ is compact in U (TG, I, p. 77, prop. 6), hence in $X'$. In particular it is a closed subset of $X'$, and $\widetilde{f}^{-1}(V) =$ $X'-f^{-1}(K)$ is an open subset of $X'$, and is therefore a neighbourhood of $x$.

Conversely, let $g: X'\rightarrow Y'$ be a continuous mapping such that $g(\omega_X) =\omega_Y$, and let $\Gamma_g\subset X'\times Y'$ be its graph. The set U = X $-\overset{-1}{g}(\omega_Y)$ is open in X. The correspondence $f= (\Gamma_g\cap (U\times Y),X,Y)$ is a proper partial mapping of X into Y (TG, I, p. 77, prop. 7) such that $\widetilde{f}=g$, and it is the only one.

We shall identify the proper partial mappings of X into Y with the continuous mappings of $X'$ into $Y'$ which map $\omega_X$ onto $\omega_Y$. In particular, the proper mappings of X into Y are the proper partial mappings with domain X; they are identified with the continuous mappings $f$ of $X'$ into $Y'$ such that that $\overset{-1}{f}(\omega_Y) =\{\omega_X\}$. If X is compact, these are simply the continuous mappings of X into Y.

Let A be a commutative complex Banach algebra. Recall that $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is identified with the compact space obtained from $\mathsf{X}(A)$ by adjoining a point at infinity (I, p. 29, corollaire).

#### Proposition 2 {#ts-i-s3-prop-2 .statement tag=027Z}

Let A and B be commutative complex Banach algebras. For every algebra morphism $\pi : A\rightarrow B$, the mapping $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )$ is a proper partial mapping of $\mathsf{X}(B)$ into $\mathsf{X}(A)$.

In fact, $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )$ is a continuous mapping of $\mathsf{X}'(B)$ into $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ (I, p. 10). The point at infinity of $\mathsf{X}'(B)$ (resp. of $\mathsf{X}'(A)$) is the null character, and one has $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )(0) = 0$.

#### Proposition 3 {#ts-i-s3-prop-3 .statement tag=0280}

a) For every proper partial mapping $\varphi$ of X into Y, the mapping $f\mapsto f\circ \varphi$ of $\mathscr{C}(Y')$ into $\mathscr{C}(X')$ induces an algebra morphism $\varphi^*$ of $\mathscr{C}_0(Y)$ into $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ ;

b) The mapping $\varphi \mapsto \varphi^*$ is a bijection of the set of proper partial mappings of X into Y onto the set of algebra morphisms of $\mathscr{C}_0(Y)$ into $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$. Its inverse bijection is the mapping $\pi \mapsto \mathsf{X}'(\pi )$.

Let us prove a). Let $\varphi$ be a proper partial mapping of X into Y, identified with a continuous mapping of $X'$ into $Y'$ such that $\varphi (\omega_X) =\omega_Y$. For $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(Y)$, one has $(f\circ \varphi )(\omega_X) =f(\omega_Y) = 0$, hence the mapping $\varphi^*$ is well-defined. It is an algebra morphism.

Let us prove that $\mathsf{X}'(\varphi^*)$ identifies with $\varphi$. Let $x\in X$. For every function $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(Y)$, the character $\mathsf{X}'(\varphi^*$)(ev$_x$) associates to $f$ the complex number

(ev$_x\circ \varphi^*$)$(f) =$ ev$_x(f\circ \varphi ) =f(\varphi (x))$,

hence $\mathsf{X}'(\varphi^*$)(ev$_x$) $=$ ev$_{\varphi(x)}$. This proves the assertion.

Conversely, let $\pi :\mathscr{C}_0(Y)\rightarrow \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ be an algebra morphism. Let us prove that $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )^*=\pi$. Let $f\in \mathscr{C}_0(Y)$, and let us write $g=\mathsf{X}'(\pi )^*(f)\in$ $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$. For every $x\in X$, one has

$g(x) = (f\circ \mathsf{X}'(\pi ))(x) =$ ev$_{\mathsf{X}'(\pi)(x)}(f) =$ (ev$_x\circ \pi$ )$(f) =\pi (f)(x)$,

since $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )$ satisfies ev$_{\mathsf{X}'(\pi)(x)}=$ ev$_x\circ \pi$. Hence $g=\pi (f)$, which permits us to conclude that $\mathsf{X}'(\pi )^*=\pi$.

In an entirely similar manner, one has:

#### Proposition 4 {#ts-i-s3-prop-4 .statement tag=0281}

Suppose that X and Y are compact. Let us identify the space X (resp. the space Y) with $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}(X))$ (resp. $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}(Y))$) (corollary 3 of I, p. 33).

a) For every continuous mapping $\varphi : X\rightarrow$ Y, the mapping $\varphi^*:f\mapsto f\circ \varphi$ is an algebra morphism of $\mathscr{C}(Y)$ into $\mathscr{C}(X)$;

b) The mappings $\varphi \mapsto \varphi^*$ and $\pi \mapsto \mathsf{X}(\pi )$ are inverse bijections between the set of continuous mappings of X into Y and the set of algebra morphisms of $\mathscr{C}(Y)$ into $\mathscr{C}(X)$.

#### Remark {#ts-i-s3-n3-rem-1 .statement tag=0282}

*In the language of category theory, the preceding results may be interpreted in the following way. Let $\mathbf{G}$ be the category whose objects are locally compact topological spaces and whose morphisms are proper partial mappings. The functor X $\mapsto \mathscr{C}_0(X)$ is a contravariant, fully faithful functor from the category $\mathbf{G}$ into the category of commutative complex Banach algebras. Moreover, $A\mapsto \mathsf{X}(A)$ is a contravariant functor from the category of commutative complex Banach algebras into the category $\mathbf{G}$. If to a locally compact topological space X one associates the homeomorphism

ev$: X\rightarrow \mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$,

one obtains an isomorphism of the identity functor of the category $\mathbf{G}$ onto the composite functor $X\mapsto \mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$.

It is not true that the composite functor $A\mapsto \mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$ is isomorphic to the identity functor of the category of commutative complex Banach algebras (cf. example 2 of I, p. 36 and exercise 2 of I, p. 155). One will, however, see a statement of this type for commutative star-algebras (No. 5 of I, p. 107).*

### 4. Gelfand Transform

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. Recall that, for every $x\in A$, $\mathscr{G}_A(x)$, or $\mathscr{G}(x)$, denotes the function $\chi \mapsto \chi (x)$ on $\mathsf{X}(A)$, that $\mathscr{G}(x)$ is called the Gelfand transform of $x$, and that the mapping $x\mapsto \mathscr{G}(x)$ is called the Gelfand transform (cf. definition 5 of I, p. 7). We have therefore by definition:

$$
\mathscr{G}(x)(\chi ) =\chi (x)
$$

#### Example 1 {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-1 .statement tag=0283}

Let X be a locally compact topological space and consider the commutative Banach algebra $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ (example 3 of I, p. 17 and No. 2 of I, p. 31). By Corollary 1 of I, p. 32, the character space $\mathsf{X}(\mathscr{C}_0(X))$ is identified with X by means of the mapping which to an element $x\in X$ associates the character $f\mapsto f(x)$ of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$, and the Gelfand transform of $\mathscr{C}_0(X)$ is then identified with the identity mapping.

#### Example 2 {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-2 .statement tag=0284}

Let $n\geqslant 0$ be an integer. Let $A_n$ be the algebra of functions $f: [0,1]\rightarrow K$ admitting continuous derivatives on $[0,1]$ up to order $n$. Endowed with the norm

$\|f\|=\sum^nk1$! sup$_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}|f^{(k)}(t)|$,

$k=0$

it is a Banach algebra (example 4 of I, p. 18). For every $n$, the character space $\mathsf{X}(A_n)$ is identified with $[0,1]$ and $\mathscr{G}$ with the inclusion of $A_n$ in $\mathscr{C}([0,1])$ (cf. example 1 of I, p. 144).

#### Example 3 {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-3 .statement tag=0285}

Let Δ be the disk of complex numbers $z$ satisfying $|z|\leqslant 1$ and let A be the complex Banach algebra of continuous functions on Δ analytic in the interior of Δ, endowed with the norm $\|f\|=$ sup$_{z\in\Delta}|f(z)|$ (example 9 of I, p. 20). Then $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is identified with Δ and $\mathscr{G}$ with the inclusion of A in $\mathscr{C}(\Delta )$ (cf. Exercise 6 of I, p. 193).

#### Example 4 {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-4 .statement tag=0286}

Consider the complex Banach algebra A of absolutely convergent Fourier series (example 8 of I, p. 19). For every element $u$ of the unit circle $\mathbf{U}$, the mapping $f\mapsto f(u)$ is a character ev$_u$ of A. If $f_0\in A$ is the identity mapping of $\mathbf{U}$, we have ev$_u(f_0) =u$, hence the mapping ev$:u\mapsto$ ev$_u$ of $\mathbf{U}$ into $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is injective; it is continuous.

Let $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$. We have $\|f_0\|$ = $\|f_0^{-1}\|$ = 1, hence $|\chi (f_0)|\leqslant$ 1 and $|\chi (f_0)^{-1}|\leqslant 1$. This shows that $\chi (f_0)\in \mathbf{U}$ and there exists $u\in \mathbf{U}$ such that $\chi (f_0) =$ ev$_u(f_0)$. Since $\{f_0, f_0^{-1}\}$ generates the unital algebra A topologically, we have $\chi =$ ev$_u$. Thus, the mapping ev is a homeomorphism of $\mathbf{U}$ onto $\mathsf{X}(A)$, by which these spaces are identified. The Gelfand transform of A is then identified with the inclusion of A in $\mathscr{C}(\mathbf{U})$.

Since A is isomorphic to the Banach algebra $L^1(\mathbf{Z})$ (example 8 of I, p. 19), the space $\mathsf{X}(L^1(\mathbf{Z}))$ is identified with $\mathbf{U}$ and, for every element $(c_n)\in L^1(\mathbf{Z})$, the Gelfand transform $\mathscr{G}_{L^1(\mathbf{Z})}((c_n))$ is identified with the function $u\mapsto \sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}c_nu^n$ on $\mathbf{U}$.

#### Example 5 {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-5 .statement tag=0287}

Let Δ be the unit disc of complex numbers $z$ such that $|z|\leqslant 1$. Its boundary in $\mathbf{C}$ is $\mathbf{U}$. Let A be the Banach algebra of complex functions $f$ on $\mathbf{U}$ such that there exists a continuous function $\widetilde{f}\in \mathscr{C}(\Delta )$ extending $f$ which is analytic in $\mathring{\Delta}$, endowed with the norm $\|f\|=$ sup$_{z\in\mathbf{U}}|f(z)|$. By virtue of the maximum principle (VAR, R1, p. 30, 3.3.7), we then have $\|f\|=$ sup$_{z\in\Delta}|\widetilde{f}(z)|$, and therefore A coincides with the algebra of example 9 of I, p. 20. The set $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is identified with Δ and, if $f\in A$, the mapping $\mathscr{G}(f)$ is identified with the continuous extension of $f$ to Δ which is analytic in $\mathring{\Delta}($cf. exerc. 6 of I, p. 193).

#### Proposition 5 {#ts-i-s3-prop-5 .statement tag=0288}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. For every $x\in A$, the function $\mathscr{G}(x)$ belongs to the commutative Banach algebra $\mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$ of continuous functions on $\mathsf{X}(A)$ tending to 0 at infinity.

By definition (cf. n$^o7$ of I, p. 9), the function $\mathscr{G}'_A(x) :\chi \mapsto \chi (x)$ is continuous on $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ and zero at 0. Since $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ is identified with the Alexandroff compactification of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ by cor. 1 of I, p. 29, the proposition follows.

#### Proposition 6 {#ts-i-s3-prop-6 .statement tag=0289}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra and let $x\in A$.

a) The union of the set of values of $\mathscr{G}(x)$ and of $\{0\}$ is equal to Sp$'_A(x)$ ;

b) If A has a unit element, the set of values of $\mathscr{G}(x)$ is Sp$_A(x)$. In particular, in order that $x$ be invertible, it is necessary and sufficient that $\mathscr{G}(x)$ should not vanish.

Suppose that A admits a unit element. It is known that, for every $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$, one has $\chi (x)\in$ Sp$_A(x)$. Conversely, let $\lambda \in$ Sp$_A(x)$. Then $x-\lambda$ is not invertible, hence belongs to a maximal ideal of A. There then exists $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$ such that $\chi (x-\lambda ) = 0$ (th. 2 of I, p. 30), whence b).

Let us pass to the general case. Let $\widetilde{A}$ be the Banach algebra obtained from A by adjoining a unit element; it is commutative. The set Sp$'_A(x)$ is equal to Sp$_{\widetilde{A}}(x)$, that is to say to the set of values of $\mathscr{G}_{\widetilde{A}}(x)$ on $\mathsf{X}(\widetilde{A}) =\mathsf{X}'(A)$. Whence a).

#### Example {#ts-i-s3-n4-exa-6 .statement tag=028A}

Consider the Banach algebra A of absolutely convergent Fourier series (Example 4). Prop. 6, b), implies that if $\varphi$ is a function on the unit circle $\mathbf{U}$ admitting an absolutely convergent Fourier series, and if $\varphi$ does not vanish, the function $1/\varphi$ also admits an absolutely convergent Fourier series ("Wiener's theorem").

#### Proposition 7 {#ts-i-s3-prop-7 .statement tag=028B}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra.

a) The Gelfand transform $\mathscr{G}$ defines a morphism of A into $\mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$ such that $\|\mathscr{G}(x)\|=\varrho (x)\leqslant \|x\|$ for every $x\in A$;

b) In order that the Gelfand transform $\mathscr{G}$ be isometric, it is necessary and sufficient that $\|x^2\|=\|x\|^2$ for every $x\in A$.

The mapping $\mathscr{G}$ is a morphism of A into $\mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$ by No. $^o7$ of I, p. 9 and Prop. 5 of I, p. 37, and satisfies $\|\mathscr{G}(x)\|=\varrho (x)$ by Prop. 6 and Cor. 5 of I, p. 28. Assertion b) follows from a) and Remark 1 of I, p. 21.

#### Corollary {#ts-i-s3-n4-cor-1 .statement tag=028C}

Let A be a Banach algebra, and let $x$ and $y$ be permutable elements of A.

a) One has $\varrho (xy)\leqslant \varrho (x)\varrho (y)$ and $\varrho (x+y)\leqslant \varrho (x) +\varrho (y)$;

b) If $y$ is quasi-nilpotent, then Sp$'_A(x) =$ Sp$'_A(x+y)$; if moreover A is unital, then Sp$_A(x) =$ Sp$_A(x+y)$.

By considering the Banach algebra deduced from A by adjoining a unit element, one is first reduced to the case where the algebra A is unital. Then by considering the closed full subalgebra of A generated by $x$ and $y$, one is reduced to the case where A is commutative and unital. Assertion a) is then a consequence of Prop. 7, a), and assertion b) follows from Prop. 6 of I, p. 37 and Cor. 5 of I, p. 28.

#### Proposition 8 {#ts-i-s3-prop-8 .statement tag=028D}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. The following four sets are equal:

(i) The kernel of the Gelfand transform;

(ii) The set of elements $x$ of A such that Sp$'_A(x) =\{0\}$;

(iii) The set of quasi-nilpotent elements of A;

(iv) The radical of A.

Let us denote these sets by $N_1,N_2,N_3,N_4$, respectively. One has $N_1=$ $N_2$ (Prop. 6, a)), and $N_2= N_3($I, p. 28, Cor. 5). By definition, the set $N_4$ is the intersection of the regular maximal ideals of A; it is therefore the intersection of the kernels of the characters of A (th. 2 of I, p. 30), which is equal to $N_1$.

#### Remark 1 {#ts-i-s3-n4-rem-1 .statement tag=028E}

In general, the image of the Gelfand transform is neither closed in $\mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$, nor dense in $\mathscr{C}_0(\mathsf{X}(A))$ (Exercise 7 of I, p. 193).

#### Remark 2 {#ts-i-s3-n4-rem-2 .statement tag=028F}

The image of the Gelfand transform separates the points of $\mathsf{X}(A)$, since if $\chi_1\not=\chi_2$ are elements of $\mathsf{X}(A)$, there exists $x\in A$ such that $\chi_1(x)\not=\chi_2(x)$.

#### Remark 3 {#ts-i-s3-n4-rem-3 .statement tag=028G}

If $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$, there exists an element of the image of the Gelfand transform which does not vanish at $\chi$.

#### Remark 4 {#ts-i-s3-n4-rem-4 .statement tag=028H}

If A possesses a unit element, the image of the Gelfand transform is a full subalgebra of the algebra of continuous functions on $\mathsf{X}(A)$ (Prop. 6, b)).

#### Lemma 2 {#ts-i-s3-lem-2 .statement tag=028I}

Let A be a commutative Banach algebra. Let M be a subset of $\mathsf{X}(A)$. Then M is closed for the Jacobson topology if and only if, for every character $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$ - M, there exists an element $x$ of A such that $\mathscr{G}(x)$ is zero on M and nonzero at $\chi$.

Let Υ(M) be the intersection of the kernels of the elements of M. The set M is closed for the Jacobson topology if and only if M = V(Υ(M)) (cf. I, p. 13 and I, p. 30). This condition is equivalent to saying that the elements $\chi$ of M are precisely the characters which vanish on Υ(M). Consequently M is closed if and only if for every character $\chi  \notin M$, there exists $x\in \Upsilon (M)$ such that $\chi (x)\not= 0$. This translates into $\mathscr{G}(x)(\chi )\not= 0$ and $\mathscr{G}(x)|M = 0$.

### 5. Morphisms of commutative Banach algebras

#### Proposition 9 {#ts-i-s3-prop-9 .statement tag=028J}

Let A be a Banach algebra, let B be a commutative Banach algebra without radical, Every morphism of the underlying algebra of A into the underlying algebra of B is continuous.

Let $h: A\rightarrow B$ be an algebra morphism and let $(a, b)\in A\times B$ be a point adherent to the graph Γ of $h$. Let $\chi \in \mathsf{X}'(B)$. The function $x\mapsto \chi (h(x))$ from A into $\mathbf{C}$ is an algebra homomorphism, hence is continuous (I, p. 29, th. 1). The map from $A\times B$ into $\mathbf{C}$ given by $(x, y)\mapsto \chi (h(x))-\chi (y)$ is therefore continuous; it is zero on Γ, hence zero at $(a, b)$. Thus one has $\chi (h(a)) =\chi (b)$ for every $\chi \in \mathsf{X}'(B)$. Since B is without radical, one has $h(a) =b$. Thus the graph of $h$ is closed and therefore $h$ is continuous (EVT, I, p. 19, cor. 5).

#### Corollary {#ts-i-s3-n5-cor-1 .statement tag=028K}

On a commutative complex algebra without radical, two norms defining Banach algebra structures are equivalent.

It is enough to apply prop. 9 to the identity map of the algebra.

Let A and B be commutative Banach algebras. By No.$^o7$ of I, p. 9, if $h: A\rightarrow B$ is a surjective morphism, $\mathsf{X}'(h)$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathsf{X}'(B)$ onto a closed subspace of $\mathsf{X}'(A)$ which transforms 0 into 0. (In the case where $h$ is the injection of a subalgebra into $\mathscr{C}(X)$, the map $\mathsf{X}'(h)$ is injective under much weaker hypotheses, cf. I, p. 142, prop. 1, d).)

Now let $h: A\rightarrow B$ be an injective morphism. In general, $\mathsf{X}'(h)$ is not surjective, but the following proposition provides a necessary condition for this to be the case.

#### Proposition 10 {#ts-i-s3-prop-10 .statement tag=028L}

Let A and B be commutative unital Banach algebras, $h: A\rightarrow B$ a unital algebra morphism, not necessarily continuous. If $\mathsf{X}(h)$ is surjective, then $h(A)$ is a full subalgebra of B.

Let $x\in A$ be such that $h(x)$ is invertible in B. For every $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$, there exists $\xi \in \mathsf{X}(B)$ such that $\chi =\mathsf{X}(h)(\xi )$, hence $\chi (x) =\xi (h(x))\not= 0$. Prop. 6 of I, p. 37 then shows that $x$ is invertible in A, and hence that $h(x)$ is invertible in $h(A)$.

The necessary condition of the proposition is not sufficient, even if $h$ is isometric (I, p. 168, exerc. 14). One has nevertheless the following result:

#### Proposition 11 {#ts-i-s3-prop-11 .statement tag=028M}

Let A and B be commutative unital Banach algebras, let $a$ be an element of A and let $h: A\rightarrow B$ be an injective unital morphism (not necessarily continuous). Suppose that the full closed subalgebra of A generated by $a$ is equal to A. The following conditions are equivalent:

(i) $\mathsf{X}(h)$ is surjective;

(ii) $h(A)$ is a full subalgebra of B;

(iii) Sp$_A(a) =$ Sp$_B(h(a))$.

(i) $=\Rightarrow$ (ii) follows from prop. 10.

(ii) $=\Rightarrow$ (iii) results from the formula Sp$_A(a) =$ Sp$_{h(A)}(h(a)) =$ Sp$_B(h(a))$, valid since $h(A)$ is a full subalgebra of B.

(iii) $=\Rightarrow$ (i) by the formula (4) of No.$^o6$ of I, p. 6, we have the commutative diagram

$$
\leftarrow^{\mathsf{X}(h)}
$$

$$
\mathsf{X}(B)\rightarrow \mathsf{X}(A)
$$

$$
\rightarrow \leftarrow_{\mathscr{G}_B(h(a))}\rightarrow \leftarrow_{\mathscr{G}_A(a)}
$$

$$
\leftarrow_i
$$

Sp$_B(h(a))\rightarrow$ Sp$_A(a)$

where the vertical arrows denote surjective mappings (I, p. 37, prop. 6) and $i$ is the canonical inclusion. Assumption (iii) means that $i$ is bijective. Moreover the surjective mapping $\mathscr{G}_A(a) :\mathsf{X}(A)\rightarrow$ Sp$_A(a)$ is bijective: in fact, for every characters $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$ of A, the set $A_{\chi_1,\chi_2}=\{x\in A|\chi_1(x) =\chi_2(x)\}$ is a closed full subalgebra of A. By hypothesis, therefore, one has $A_{\chi_1,\chi_2}$ = A if $a\in A_{\chi_1,\chi_2}$, that is to say $\chi_1=\chi_2$ if $\mathscr{G}_A(a)\chi_1=\mathscr{G}_A(a)\chi_2$. The diagram then implies that the mapping $\mathsf{X}(h)$ is surjective.

### 6. Simultaneous Spectrum

Let Λ be a set. Let $C_{\Lambda}=\mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$ be the unital algebra of complex polynomials in a family of indeterminates $(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$. For every $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(C_{\Lambda})$, one has $(\chi (X_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$; the mapping $\chi \mapsto$ $(\chi (X_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathsf{X}(C_{\Lambda})$ onto the product space $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$, by means of which these spaces are identified.

Let, on the other hand, A be a commutative unital Banach algebra and $x= (x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ a family of elements of A. There exists one and only one unital morphism $h$ of $C_{\Lambda}$ into A such that $h(X_{\lambda}) =x_{\lambda}$ for every $\lambda$. The continuous mapping $\mathsf{X}(h)$ of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ into $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ is the mapping which associates to $\chi$ the family $(\chi (x_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$. It is called the mapping of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ into $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ defined by $x$.

#### Definition 2 {#ts-i-s3-def-2 .statement tag=028N}

The image of the mapping $\mathsf{X}(h)$ is called the simultaneous spectrum of $x$, and denoted by Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ or Sp$^{\Lambda}(x)$.

The simultaneous spectrum of $x$ is a compact subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. An element $c= (c_{\lambda})\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ belong to Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ if and only if the elements $x_{\lambda}-c_{\lambda}$ belong to one and the same maximal ideal of A, in other words if the family $(x_{\lambda}-c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ does not generate the algebra A.

If Λ contains only one element, so that the family $x$ reduces to a single element $x\in A$, we have Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x) =$ Sp$_A(x)$ (I, p. 37, prop. 6, b)). If $\Lambda '\subset \Lambda$, then Sp$^{\Lambda'}_A((x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda'})$ is the image of Sp$^{\Lambda}_A((x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda})$ under the canonical projection mapping of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ onto $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda'}$. In particular, we have

Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)\subset \prod_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ Sp$_A(x_{\lambda})$.

Let $z_{\lambda}$, for $\lambda \in \Lambda$, denote the coordinate functions on $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. If $\chi \in$ $\mathsf{X}(A)$, the value at $\chi$ of $z_{\lambda}\circ \mathsf{X}(h)$ is $\chi (x_{\lambda})$, hence $z_{\lambda}\circ \mathsf{X}(h) =\mathscr{G}(x_{\lambda})$.

Let A and B be commutative unital Banach algebras, $\varphi$ a unital morphism from A into B, and $x= (x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ a family of elements of A. Let $\varphi (x)$ denote the family $(\varphi (x_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ of elements of B. We have, for every $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(B)$, and every $\lambda \in \Lambda$

$$
\chi (\varphi (x_{\lambda})) = (\mathsf{X}(\varphi )(\chi ))(x_{\lambda})
$$

hence Sp$^{\Lambda}_B(\varphi (x))\subset$ Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$. The diagram

$$
\leftarrow^{\mathsf{X}(\varphi)}
$$

$$
\mathsf{X}(B)\rightarrow \mathsf{X}(A)
$$

$$
\rightarrow \leftarrow \rightarrow \leftarrow \tag{1}
$$

$$
_{\Lambda}\leftarrow_{i\Lambda}
$$

Sp$_B(\varphi (x))\rightarrow$ Sp$_A(x)$

where $i$ denotes the inclusion, and where the vertical arrows denote the mappings defined by the families $\varphi (x)$ and $x$, is therefore commutative.

#### Example {#ts-i-s3-n6-exa-1 .statement tag=028O}

Let K $\subset \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ be a compact subset. Let $z= (z_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ be the family in $\mathscr{C}(K)$ of the restrictions to K of the coordinate functions of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. Then the joint spectrum Sp$^{\Lambda}_{\mathscr{C}(K)}(z)$ is equal to K. In fact, by Corollary 2 of I, p. 32, every character $\chi$ of $\mathscr{C}(K)$ is of the form $f\mapsto f(x)$ for an element $x\in K$, and one then has $(\chi (z_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}=x$.

#### Proposition 12 {#ts-i-s3-prop-12 .statement tag=028P}

Let Λ be a set and A a commutative unital Banach algebra. Let $x= (x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ be a family of elements of A.

a) Suppose that the full subalgebra of A generated by the family $x$ is dense in A. The mapping of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ into $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ defined by $x$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ onto the joint spectrum Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ ;

b) Suppose that the unital subalgebra of A generated by the family $x$ is dense in A. For every $c\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$, the following conditions are equivalent :

(i) $c\in$ Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ ;

(ii) $|P(c)|\leqslant \varrho (P(x))$ for every polynomial $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$;

(iii) $|P(c)|\leqslant \|P(x)\|$ for every polynomial $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$.

a) The mapping of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ into Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ defined by the family $x$ is continuous and surjective. Let $\chi ,\chi '\in \mathsf{X}(A)$ be characters having the same image, that is, such that $\chi (x_{\lambda}) =\chi '(x_{\lambda})$ for every $\lambda \in \Lambda$. The characters $\chi$ and $\chi '$ coincide on elements of the form $P(x)Q(x)^{-1}$, where $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$, $Q\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$ and $Q(x)$ is invertible in A, that is, on the full subalgebra of A generated by the elements $x_{\lambda}$ (lemma 2 of I, p. 6). Since $\chi$ and $\chi '$ are continuous (Theorem 1 of I, p. 29), they are therefore equal on A. This proves that $\mathsf{X}(h)$ is a continuous bijection of $\mathsf{X}(A)$ onto Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$, and hence is a homeomorphism since $\mathsf{X}(A)$ is compact.

b) Let us show that (i) implies (ii): if $c= (c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in$ Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$, there exists $\chi \in \mathsf{X}(A)$ such that $c_{\lambda}=\chi (x_{\lambda})$ for every $\lambda$. For every $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$, we therefore have $|P(c)|=|P((\chi (x_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda})|=|\chi (P(x))|\leqslant \varrho (P(x))$.

Assertion (ii) implies (iii) by reason of the inequality $\varrho (x)\leqslant \|x\|$, valid for every $x\in A$ by definition of the spectral radius.

Finally let us show that (iii) implies (i). Let $c= (c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ be such that

$$
|P(c)|\leqslant \|P(x)\| \tag{2}
$$

for every P $\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$. Let $A'$ be the unital subalgebra of A generated by the family $x$; its elements are of the form $P(x)$ for $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$. The majorisation (2) implies that the condition $P(x) = 0$ entails $P(c) = 0$. There therefore exists a morphism of unital algebras $\xi$ from $A'$ into $\mathbf{C}$ such that $\xi (x_{\lambda}) =c_{\lambda}$ for every $\lambda \in \Lambda$. By (2), the morphism $\xi$ is continuous. It therefore extends by continuity to a character $\chi$ of $\overline{A'}= A$, which satisfies $c= (\chi (x_{\lambda}))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\in$ Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$. This completes the proof.

### 7. Polynomially convex sets

#### Definition 3 {#ts-i-s3-def-3 .statement tag=028Q}

Let Λ be a set and V a subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. One says that V is polynomially convex if V is the set of points $(c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ such that

$|P((c_{\lambda}))|\leqslant$ sup$_{c\in V}|P(c)|$

for every $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$.

#### Lemma 3 {#ts-i-s3-lem-3 .statement tag=028R}

Let $\Lambda$ be a set. A subset V of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ is polynomially convex if and only if there exist a family $(P_i)_{i\in I}$ of elements of $\mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$ and a family $(M_i)_{i\in I}$ of elements of $[0,+\infty ]$ such that V is the set of $c\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ satisfying

$$
|P_i(c)|\leqslant M_i
$$

for all $i\in I$.

If the subset V of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ is polynomially convex, it satisfies the above condition for the family consisting of the elements P of $\mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$ by setting $M_P=$ sup$_{c\in V}|P(c)|$.

Conversely, let $(P_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of elements of $\mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}]$ and $(M_i)_{i\in I}$ a family of elements of $[0,+\infty ]$. Let V be the set of $c$ in $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ such that $|P_i(c)|\leqslant M_i$ for all $i\in I$. Then sup$_{c\in V}|P_i(c)|\leqslant M_i$ for $i\in I$. Suppose that $x\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ satisfies

$|P(x)|\leqslant$ sup$_{c\in V}|P(c)|$

for all $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$. For $i\in I$, we have in particular $|P_i(x)|\leqslant M_i$, hence $x\in V$. Conversely, for every element $x\in V$ and every polynomial $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$, we have $|P(x)|\leqslant$ sup$_{c\in V}|P(c)|$. Consequently, the set V is polynomially convex.

#### Lemma 4 {#ts-i-s3-lem-4 .statement tag=028S}

Let A be a commutative unital Banach algebra. Let $\Lambda$ be a set and $x= (x_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ a family of elements of A. If the unital subalgebra generated by the family $x$ is dense in A, then the simultaneous spectrum Sp$^{\Lambda}_A(x)$ is polynomially convex.

This follows from assertion b) of Prop. 12 of I, p. 43 and Definition 3.

Any intersection of polynomially convex subsets of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ is polynomially convex (Lemma 3). This justifies the following definition:

#### Definition 4 {#ts-i-s3-def-4 .statement tag=028T}

Let $\Lambda$ be a set and V a subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. The polynomially convex envelope of V is the smallest polynomially convex subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ containing V.

The polynomially convex envelope of V is the set of $c$ belonging to $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ such that $|P(c)|\leqslant$ sup$_W|P|$ for all $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$. In fact, this set is polynomially convex by Lemma 3, and is contained in every polynomially convex set containing V by definition.

#### Example {#ts-i-s3-n7-exa-1 .statement tag=028U}

Let $\Lambda$ be a finite set and $V\subset \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ a compact convex subset. Then V is polynomially convex. In fact, let W be the polynomially convex envelope of V. Let us prove that $W\subset V$, which will imply the assertion. Let $x\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}-$V. There exists a real hyperplane H in $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ which strictly separates $x$ and V (EVT, II, p. 41, Prop. 4). Let $f_{\mathbf{R}}$ be an $\mathbf{R}$-linear form on $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ and $\alpha \in \mathbf{R}$ such that H is the set of $y\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ satisfying $f_{\mathbf{R}}(y) =\alpha$. Let $f$ be a linear form on $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ such that $f_{\mathbf{R}}=\mathscr{R}(f)$. We therefore have

$\mathscr{R}(f(x))>$ sup$_{y\in V}\mathscr{R}(f(y))$.

For every $t\in \mathbf{R}$ and $y\in V$, let us set $f_t(y) =t+f(y)$. We have $|f_t|-\mathscr{R}(f_t)\rightarrow 0$ in $\mathscr{C}(\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda},\mathbf{R})$ endowed with the topology of compact convergence when $t\rightarrow +\infty$. For $t$ sufficiently large, it follows that $|f_t(x)|>$ sup$_{y\in V}|f_t(y)|$ since V is compact. Thus $x\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}-$ W since $f_t$ is a polynomial function.

#### Lemma 5 {#ts-i-s3-lem-5 .statement tag=028V}

Let K be a compact subset of $\mathbf{C}$. We denote by $\widehat{K}$ the union of K and of the connected components of $\mathbf{C}-$ K which are relatively compact. Then the set $\widehat{K}$ is compact.

Since K is compact, there exists a real number $r >0$ such that K is contained in the open disk D with center 0 and radius $r$. Then $\mathbf{C}-$ K contains $\mathbf{C}-$D. Since the space $\mathbf{C}-$ D is connected (being homeomorphic to $[r,+\infty [\times \mathbf{S}^1)$, it is contained in a connected component U of $\mathbf{C}^-$K. Every other connected component of $\mathbf{C}^-$ K is contained in D, hence is bounded. The connected component U is therefore the unique unbounded connected component of $\mathbf{C}^-$ K, that is, $U =\mathbf{C}-\widehat{K}$. Since U is open and contains the complement of the disk D, the set $\widehat{K}$ is compact.

#### Proposition 13 {#ts-i-s3-prop-13 .statement tag=028W}

Let $n\geqslant 1$ be an integer. Let $K\subset \mathbf{C}^n$ be a closed subset and V its polynomially convex hull.

a) Every bounded connected component of $\mathbf{C}^n-$ K is contained in V;

b) If $n= 1$, and if K is compact, then V is the union $\widehat{K}$ of K and of the bounded connected components of $\mathbf{C}-$ K.

Since $K\subset \mathbf{C}^n$ is closed, its complement $\mathbf{C}^n-$ K is open, hence locally connected, so that each connected component of $\mathbf{C}^n-$ K is open. The maximum principle (VAR, R1, p. 29, 3.3.7) then shows that every bounded connected component of $\mathbf{C}^n-$ K is contained in V, which proves assertion a).

Now suppose $n= 1$ and K compact. Let us denote by $\widehat{K}$ the union of K and of the bounded connected components of $\mathbf{C}-$ K, so that $\widehat{K}\subset$ V by what precedes. The set $\widehat{K}$ is compact (Lemma 5). Let A be the commutative unital Banach algebra $\mathscr{C}(\widehat{K})$. Let $x\in A$ be the identity function of $\widehat{K}$ and B the closed unital subalgebra of A generated by $x$. We have Sp$_A(x) =\widehat{K}$ (Example 3 of I, p. 17), hence $\mathbf{C}-$ Sp$_A(x)$ is connected. Consequently we have Sp$_B(x) =$ Sp$_A(x)$ (Cor. of Prop. 6 of I, p. 28). Since Sp$_B(x)$ is polynomially convex by Lemma 4, this shows that $\widehat{K}$ is polynomially convex and therefore that $V\subset \widehat{K}$.

The second part of the proposition does not extend to the case $n\geqslant 2$ (cf. Exercise 23 of I, p. 170).

#### Proposition 14 {#ts-i-s3-prop-14 .statement tag=028X}

according to Proposition 7 of I, p. 38, a) and Proposition 12 of I, p. 43, a). The result then follows from Lemma 3.

Let Λ be a set. Let K be a polynomially convex compact subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$. Let $A_0$ be the set of the restrictions to K of polynomial functions on $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$, and let A be the closure of $A_0$ in the algebra $\mathscr{C}(K)$.

$x\in \mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ such that

$|P(x)|\leqslant$ sup$_{c\in K}|P(c)|$.

$$
D\cap \bigcap_PK_P= K \tag{3}
$$

which is contained in U. Thus, the space D is the union of the open set $D\cap U$ and the family of open sets D - $K_P$ for $P\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$. Since D is compact, there exists an integer $q\geqslant 1$ and polynomials $P_1, . . . ,P_q\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})]$ such that:

$$
D\cap K_{P_1}\cap  \cdots  \cap K_{P_q}\subset U \tag{4}
$$

There exists a finite subset $\Lambda_0\subset$ Λ such that $P_i\in \mathbf{C}[(X_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda_0}]$ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant q$. Let us prove that $\Lambda_0$ verifies the assertion of the lemma.

Let $\Lambda '$ be a subset of Λ containing $\Lambda_0$. Let E be the subset of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda'}$ formed by the elements $c= (c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda'}$ defined by the inequalities $|c_{\lambda}|\leqslant R_{\lambda}$ for $\lambda \in \Lambda '$, and

$|P_i(c)|\leqslant$ sup$_{c\in K}|P_i(c)|$

for $i= 1, . . . ,q$. The subset E is polynomially convex (lemma 3) and formula (3) proves that pr$_{\Lambda'}(K)\subset E$.

On the other hand, let $c= (c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda'}\in E$; let $d= (d_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$ be the element of $\mathbf{C}^{\Lambda}$ defined by $d_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}$ for $\lambda \in \Lambda '$ and $d_{\lambda}= 0$ for $\lambda \in \Lambda$- $\Lambda '$. Then (4) implies that $d\in U$, and hence that $c\in$ pr$_{\Lambda'}(U)$. Thus, $E\subset$ pr$_{\Lambda'}(U)$, which completes the proof.

#### Lemma 7 {#ts-i-s3-lem-7 .statement tag=0290}

Let $n\geqslant 1$ be an integer and K a compact polynomially convex subset of $\mathbf{C}^n$. Then K admits a fundamental system of compact polynomially convex neighbourhoods.

There exists a compact polydisk (cf. VAR, R1, p. 24) Δ of $\mathbf{C}^n$ which is a neighbourhood of K. Since K is polynomially convex, there exists a family $(P_i)_{i\in I}$ of elements of $\mathbf{C}[X_1, . . . ,X_n]$, and a family $(M_i)_{i\in I}$ of positive real numbers such that K is the set of the $z\in$ Δ verifying $|P_i(z)|\leqslant M_i$ for every $i$ (lemma 3). For every finite subset J of I and every $\varepsilon  >0$, let $K_{J,\varepsilon}$ be the set of the $z\in \Delta$ such that $|P_i(z)|\leqslant M_i+\varepsilon$ for $i\in J$. Then each set $K_{J,\varepsilon}$ is a compact polynomially convex neighbourhood of K (loc. cit.), and the intersection of the sets $K_{J,\varepsilon}$ is K. The sets $K_{J,\varepsilon}$ therefore form a fundamental system of polynomially convex neighbourhoods of K (TG, I, p. 60, th. 1).
