$ a) $ Let $ G $ be a lattice-ordered commutative group. For each $ x \geq 0 $ in $ G $, let $ I(x) $ denote the interval $[ -x, x ]$ in $ G $. A non-empty family $ (c_\alpha) $ of elements $ \geq 0 $ in $ G $ is such that the intervals $ I(c_\alpha) $ form a fundamental system of neighbourhoods of o for a topology $ \mathcal{T} $ on $ G $ compatible with the group structure of $ G $ if and only if the set of $ c_\alpha $ is directed (with respect to the relation $ \geqslant $) and for each $ \alpha $ there exists $ \beta $ such that $ 2c_\beta \leqslant c_\alpha $. If these conditions are satisfied, the mapping $ x \to x^+ $ of $ G $ into $ G $ is uniformly continuous. $ \mathcal{T} $ is Hausdorff if and only if $ \inf c_\alpha = 0 $; and in this case $ \mathcal{T} $ is compatible with the ordered group structure of $ G $.

$ b) $ On the group $ G = \mathbf{Z}^N $, the product of a countable infinity of linearly ordered groups $ \mathbf{Z} $, there is no non-discrete topology defined by the procedure of $ a) $, but the product of the discrete topologies on the factors $ \mathbf{Z} $ is a topology on $ G $, compatible with the ordered group structure of $ G $, which is Hausdorff and not discrete.

$ c) $ On a linearly ordered group $ G $ the only Hausdorff topology defined by the procedure of $ a) $ is the topology $ \mathcal{T}_0(G) $ (Chapter I, § 2, Exercise 5).

§ 3) Let $ G $ be a lattice-ordered commutative group, and let $ M $ be the semigroup of *major* subsets of $ G $. Every subset of $ M $ which is bounded above (resp. below) has a least upper bound (resp. greatest lower bound), and $ G $ can be canonically identified with a subset of $ M $. Let $ G' $ denote the largest subgroup of $ M $ (the set of all symmetrizable elements of $ M $).

$ a) $ Consider a topology $ \mathcal{T} $ on $ G $ defined by the process of Exercise 2 $ a) $; let $ V_\alpha $ be the set of all pairs $ (z, z') $ in $ M \times M $ such that

$$
z - c_\alpha \leqslant z' \leqslant z + c_\alpha;
$$

show that the $ V_\alpha $ form a fundamental system of entourages of a uniformity $ \mathcal{U} $ on $ M $, and that the topology $ \mathcal{T}' $ induced by $ \mathcal{U} $ induces $ \mathcal{T} $ on $ G $. If $ \mathcal{T} $ is Hausdorff, then $ \mathcal{U} $ is a Hausdorff uniformity (remark that the elements of $ M $, considered as subsets of $ G $, are then *closed*), and the uniform space $ M $ thus defined is *complete*. Show that the mapping $ (z, z') \to z + z' $ of $ M \times M $ into $ M $ is uniformly continuous. Supposing that $ \mathcal{T} $ is Hausdorff, show that in $ M $ the set of upper (resp. lower) bounds of any subset of $ M $ is closed with respect to $ \mathcal{T}' $ (prove this first for the set of upper or lower bounds of an element of $ M $, by considering the elements of $ M $ as subsets of $ D $), and that the mapping

$$
(z, z') \to \inf (z, z')
$$

of $ M \times M $ into $ M $ is uniformly continuous (same method).

$ b) $ Suppose from now on in this exercise that $ \mathcal{T} $ is Hausdorff. Then the closure $ \overline{G} $ of $ G $ in $ M $ is a lattice-ordered group which is complete in the topology induced by $ \mathcal{T}' $. The group $ G' $ is closed in $ M $ (remark that its closure is a subgroup of $ M $) and the topology induced by $ \mathcal{T}' $ on $ G' $ is compatible with the ordered group structure of $ G' $. For $ G' $ to be equal to $ \overline{G} $ it is sufficient that every non-empty open interval $ ]a, b[ $ in $ G' $ should contain an element of $ G $, and this is always the case if $ G $ is linearly ordered. In this case the topology induced on $ G' $ by $ \mathcal{T}' $ is $ \mathcal{T}_0(G') $.

c) Take $ G $ to be the ordered group $ Q^2 $, the product of the linearly ordered group $ Q $ by itself; then $ G' = M = R^2 $ with the product ordering. Show that there are only three distinct non-discrete Hausdorff topologies on $ G $ defined by the procedure of Exercise 2 a), and that they are obtained by taking the set of elements $ c_\alpha $ to be either the set of pairs $ (x, y) $ such that $ x > 0 $ and $ y > 0 $, or the set of pairs $ (x, 0) $ such that $ x > 0 $, or the set of pairs $ (0, y) $ such that $ y > 0 $. For the first of these three topologies we have $ \overline{G} = G' $, although there are non-empty open intervals of $ G' $ which contain no element of $ G $; for the other two topologies, we have $ \overline{G} \neq G' $. For each of these three topologies, the set of elements $ z > 0 $ in $ G $ is not open.

d) Take $ G $ to be the group $ Q^2 $ endowed with the lexicographic order (Set Theory, Chapter III, § 2, no. 6); $ G $ is a non-Archimedean linearly ordered group. Then $ \overline{G} = G' \neq M $; $ M $ is linearly ordered but the topology $ \mathcal{T}' $ on $ M $ is distinct from the topology $ \mathcal{T}_0(M) $; $ G' $ is isomorphic to the group $ R \times Q $ with the lexicographic order; in $ G' $ the subgroup $ H = R \times \{0\} $ is open and isolated, but on the quotient group $ G'/H $ the quotient topology is distinct from $ \mathcal{T}_0(G'/H) $.

§ 4) a) Let $ E $ be a linearly ordered set. Let $ F $ be the $ \mathbf{Z} $-module of formal finite linear combinations of elements of $ E $ with coefficients in $ \mathbf{Z} $, and define a linearly ordered group structure on $ F $ by taking the set $ F_+ $ of elements $ \geqslant 0 $ to be the set consisting of $ 0 $ and all linear combinations $ \sum_{\xi \in E} n(\xi) \xi \neq 0 $ such that $ n(\xi) > 0 $ for the largest element $ \xi \in E $ such that $ n(\xi) \neq 0 $. Show that $ E $ can be identified with a cofinal subset of $ F_+ $.

b) Suppose that $ E $ is well-ordered, and consider the group $ G $ of bounded mappings of $ E $ into $ F $. Define a linearly ordered group structure on $ G $ by taking the set $ G_+ $ of elements $ \geqslant 0 $ to be the set consisting of $ 0 $ and all bounded mappings $ x $ of $ E $ into $ F $ such that $ x(\xi) > 0 $ for the smallest $ \xi \in E $ such that $ x(\xi) \neq 0 $ (lexicographic ordering). Show that $ G $ is complete with respect to the topology $ \mathcal{T}_0(G) $; if moreover $ E $ is uncountable and is such that every segment $ ]-, \xi] \ (\xi \in E) $ is countable, then every countable intersection of open sets in $ G $ is open, and every compact subset of $ G $ is finite (cf. Chapter I, § 9, Exercise 4).

c) Suppose henceforth that E is well-ordered and uncountable but that every segment ]←, ξ] is countable. For each ξ ∈ E, let c_ξ be the constant mapping of E into E whose value at every point is ξ. The c_ξ form a cofinal subset in G_+. Let E_0 be a set obtained by adjoining an element ω to E, and consider the topology 𝒯, on the set X = G × E_0, generated by the following sets: (i) V_{a, b, ξ} = ]a, b[ × {ξ} for a < b in G and ξ ∈ E; (ii) W_{a, b, ξ} — for a < b in G and ξ ∈ E such that c_ξ > sup (|a|, |b|) — consisting of the pairs (x, ω) such that a < x < b, and the pairs (x, ζ) such that ζ ∈ E, ζ ≥ ξ and either a < x < b or 4c_ζ + a < x < 4c_ζ + b. Show that 𝒯 is Hausdorff and that every compact subset of X (with respect to 𝒯) is finite. Moreover G operates continuously on X according to the law (x, (y, ζ)) → (x + y, ζ); but G does not operate properly on X, although conditions a), b), c) and d) of Chapter III, § 4, no. 2, Proposition 4 are satisfied and although, for each pair of compact subsets K, L of X, the set P(K, L) (Chapter III, § 4, no. 5, Theorem 1) is compact.
