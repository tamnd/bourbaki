**(a)** Suppose first that $n=1$. If $r\in R$ is non-zero, then $r(M)$ is a non-zero submodule of $M$ and is therefore equal to $M$. Thus $r$ is surjective. Since $r$ is nilpotent, this is impossible. Hence every element of $R$ is zero.

Suppose now that $n>1$. If every element of $R$ is zero, any submodule of $M$ distinct from $0$ and $M$ will do. We may therefore suppose that there exists an element $s\in R$ with $s\neq0$. Choose such an element $s$ for which the length of $s(M)$ is minimum.

For every $r\in R$, the endomorphism $srs$ belongs to $R$. We shall prove that $srs=0$. The image of $srs$ is contained in $s(M)$. If $srs\neq0$, then the image of $srs$ is a non-zero submodule of $s(M)$. If it were equal to $s(M)$, then
$$
srs(M)=s(M).
$$
Put $S=s(M)$. The equality above gives
$$
S=s(r(S)).
$$
The module $r(S)$ is an image of the finite length module $S$, and hence
$$
\operatorname{length}(r(S))\leqslant \operatorname{length}(S).
$$
On the other hand, the restriction of $s$ to $r(S)$ is a surjective map onto $S$, so
$$
\operatorname{length}(S)\leqslant \operatorname{length}(r(S)).
$$
Consequently these lengths are equal, and the restriction of $s$ to $r(S)$ is an isomorphism. In particular,
$$
(sr)(S)=s(r(S))=S.
$$
But $sr\in R$, and $sr$ is nilpotent. Its restriction to the finite length module $S$ is therefore a surjective nilpotent endomorphism, which is impossible. Hence $srs(M)\neq s(M)$.

It follows that, if $srs\neq0$, then $srs(M)$ is a non-zero proper submodule of $s(M)$. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{length}((srs)(M))<\operatorname{length}(s(M)).
$$
This contradicts the choice of $s$. Therefore
$$
srs=0
$$
for every $r\in R$.

If $Rs=\{0\}$, we take $N=s(M)$. This submodule is non-zero, since $s\neq0$. It is distinct from $M$, since otherwise $s$ would be surjective, which is impossible for a non-zero nilpotent endomorphism. Moreover, for every $r\in R$ we have
$$
r(N)=r(s(M))=0,
$$
so that $N$ is stable under all elements of $R$.

Assume now that $Rs\neq\{0\}$ and put
$$
N=\sum_{r\in R}r(s(M)).
$$
This submodule is stable under all elements of $R$, because for $r,r'\in R$ one has
$$
r'(r(s(M)))=(r'r)(s(M))\subset N.
$$
It is non-zero because $Rs\neq\{0\}$. Finally, for every $r\in R$ we have
$$
s(r(s(M)))=srs(M)=0,
$$
and therefore
$$
N\subset\ker s.
$$
Since $s\neq0$, we have $\ker s\neq M$, and hence $N\neq M$. Thus in all cases there exists a submodule $N$ distinct from $0$ and $M$ which is stable under all elements of $R$.

**(b)** We prove the assertion by induction on the length $n$ of $M$. If $n=1$, the assertion follows from (a), since every element of $R$ is zero.

Suppose that $n>1$. By (a), there exists a proper non-zero submodule $N$ of $M$ stable under all elements of $R$. Let $m$ be the length of $N$. The endomorphisms induced by the elements of $R$ on $M/N$ are again nilpotent endomorphisms, and the set of these induced endomorphisms is stable under composition. By the induction hypothesis applied to $M/N$, there exists a Jordan--Hölder series
$$
M/N=\overline{M}_0\supset \overline{M}_1\supset\cdots\supset \overline{M}_{n-m}=0
$$
such that the induced endomorphism defined by every $r\in R$ sends $\overline{M}_i$ into $\overline{M}_{i+1}$.

Taking inverse images in $M$, we obtain a series
$$
M\supset M_1\supset\cdots\supset M_{n-m}=N
$$
with the same property. Applying the induction hypothesis to $N$, we obtain a Jordan--Hölder series
$$
N=M_{n-m}\supset M_{n-m+1}\supset\cdots\supset M_n=0
$$
such that $r(M_i)\subset M_{i+1}$ for every $r\in R$. Joining the two series gives a Jordan--Hölder series
$$
M=M_0\supset M_1\supset\cdots\supset M_n=0
$$
satisfying
$$
r(M_i)\subset M_{i+1}
$$
for every $r\in R$ and $0\leqslant i\leqslant n-1$.

Let now $(r_i)_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}$ be a sequence of elements of $R$. Since
$$
r_n(M_0)\subset M_1,\quad
r_{n-1}r_n(M_0)\subset M_2,\quad\ldots,\quad
r_1\cdots r_n(M_0)\subset M_n,
$$
we obtain
$$
r_1\cdots r_n(M)=0.
$$
Hence
$$
r_1\cdots r_n=0.
$$
