(1) If D is a proof of $A\{\lambda\}$, where $A\{x\}$ is a relation in $\mathscr{T}$, and $\lambda$ is a definite integer (that is, a term in $\mathscr{T}$ which is an integer), then

$$
P\{\lambda,g(A\{x\}),h(D)\}
$$

is a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$.

(2) If the definite integer $\mu$ is not of the form $h(D)$, or if $\mu=h(D)$ and D is not a proof $A\{\lambda\}$, then (not $P\{\lambda,g(A\{x\}),\mu\}$) is a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$.

Now let $S\{x\}$ be the relation (not $(\exists z)P\{x,x,z\}$), and let $\gamma=g(S\{x\})$, which is a term in $\mathscr{T}$. If $\mathscr{T}$ is not contradictory, there is no proof of the proposition $S\{\gamma\}$ in $\mathscr{T}$. For if D were such a proof, then

$$
P\{\gamma,g(S\{x\}),h(D)\}
$$

would be a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$. This relation is just $P\{\gamma,\gamma,h(D)\}$, and consequently $(\exists z)P\{\gamma,\gamma,z\}$ would also be a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$; but since the last relation is equivalent to (not $S\{\gamma\}$), $\mathscr{T}$ would be contradictory. Furthermore, what has just been said shows that, for each definite integer $\mu$, (not $P\{\gamma,\gamma,\mu\}$) is a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$. It follows that there is *no proof* in $\mathscr{T}$ of the proposition (not $S\{\gamma\}$), for this relation is equivalent to $(\exists z)P\{\gamma,\gamma,z\}$, and the existence of an integer $\mu$ such that $P\{\gamma,\gamma,\mu\}$ would imply that $\mathscr{T}$ was contradictory, by virtue of the preceding argument [^2]. This metamathematical theorem of Gödel has been subsequently generalized in various directions ([48], Chapter XI) [^3].

[^1]: The detailed description of $g(A)$, $h(D)$, and $P\{x,y,z\}$ is extremely long and tedious, and to write out $P\{x,y,z\}$ would require so large a number of signs as to be impossible in practice. It is this type of difficulty that we discussed in the Introduction, but no mathematician would consider that this diminishes in any way the validity of these constructions.

[^2]: In fact, the last part of this argument presupposes a little more than the consistency of $\mathscr{T}$, namely what is called the “$\omega$-consistency” of $\mathscr{T}$. This means that there is no relation $R\{x\}$ in $\mathscr{T}$ such that $R\{x\}$ implies $x\in\mathbf{N}$ and such that, for each *definite integer* $\mu$, $R\{\mu\}$ is a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$, although $(\exists x)(x\in\mathbf{N}$ and (not $R\{x\}))$ is also a theorem in $\mathscr{T}$. However, Rosser has shown that Gödel’s argument can be modified so as to require only the consistency of $\mathscr{T}$ ([48], p. 208).

[^3]: The reader may have noticed the analogy between Gödel’s argument and the sophism of the liar : the proposition $S\{\gamma\}$ implies its own falsity when interpreted in metamathematical terms! It should also be noted that the proposition $(\forall z)((z\in\mathbf{N})\Rightarrow(\text{not }P\{\gamma,\gamma,z\}))$ is intuitively true, because we have a proof of "not $P \{ \gamma, \gamma, \mu \}$" in $\mathscr{T}$ for each *definite* integer $\mu$. Nevertheless this proposition is unprovable in $\mathscr{T}$. The above situation should be compared with a result obtained earlier by Lowenheim and Skolem (see [39]). The latter defines metamathematically a relation between two *natural integers* $x$, $y$ which, when written as $x \in y$, satisfies von Neumann's axioms of set theory. Hence, at first sight, we have a new "paradox", because in this "model" all the infinite sets are countable, contrary to Cantor's inequality $\mathfrak{m} < 2^{\mathfrak{m}}$. But, in fact, the relation defined by Skolem cannot be written in formalized set theory any more than the "theorem" asserting that the set of subsets of an infinite set has only a countable infinity of "elements". At bottom, this "paradox" is only a more subtle form of the banal remark that one can write down only a finite number of assemblies in a formalized theory, and that it is therefore absurd to conceive of an uncountable set of terms of the theory — a remark parallel to that which had already led to "Richard's paradox". Similar arguments show that the formalization of set theory is indispensable if we wish to preserve the essentials of Cantor's edifice. Mathematicians are in agreement that there is hardly more than a superficial concordance between our "intuitive" conceptions of the notion of set or of integer, and the formalisms which are supposed to account for them; the disagreement begins with the question of choosing between them.
