§ Let $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ be a covering of a set A, and let $f$ be a mapping of A onto a set B. The family $(f \langle X_i \rangle)_{i \in I}$ is then a covering of B (Proposition 3), called the image under $f$ of the covering $(X_i)_{i \in I}$. If $g$ is a mapping of a set C into the set A, the family $(g^{-1} \langle X_i \rangle)_{i \in I}$ is a covering of C, called the inverse image under $g$ of the covering $(X_i)_{i \in I}$.

§ Let E and F be sets, let $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ be a covering of E, and let $(Y_\chi)_{\chi \in K}$ be a covering of F. The family $(X_i \times Y_\chi)_{(i, \chi) \in I \times K}$ is then a covering of $E \times F$, called the product of the coverings $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ of E and $(Y_\chi)_{\chi \in K}$ of F.

PROPOSITION 7. (1) Let E be a set and $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ a covering of E. If f and g are two functions with domain E such that f and g agree on $E \cap X_i$ for each $i \in I$, then f and g agree on E.

(2) Let $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of sets and let $(f_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of mappings with the same target F such that for each $i \in I$ the domain of $f_i$ is $X_i$, and for each pair $(i, \chi) \in I \times I$, $f_i$ and $f_\chi$ agree on $X_i \cap X_\chi$. Then there is exactly one function f with domain $A = \bigcup_{i \in I} X_i$ and target F which extends each of the functions $f_i$ ($i \in I$).

(1) Let x be any element of E. Then there exists $i \in I$ such that $x \in X_i$, whence $f(x) = g(x)$ by hypothesis.

(2) Let $G_i$ be the graph of $f_i$ and let $G = \bigcup_{i \in I} G_i$; let us show that G is a functional graph. If $(x, y) \in G$ and $(x, y') \in G$, there exist two indices $i, \chi$ in I such that $(x, y) \in G_i$ and $(x, y') \in G_\chi$. This implies $x \in X_i, x \in X_\chi, y = f_i(x), y' = f_\chi(x)$; but since $x \in X_i \cap X_\chi$, we have
$$
f_i(x) = f_\chi(x),
$$
that is to say, $y = y'$. The graph G has domain $\operatorname{pr}_1 G = \bigcup_{i \in I} \operatorname{pr}_1 G_i = A$; the function $f = (G, A, F)$ therefore satisfies the required conditions. Its uniqueness follows from the first part of the Proposition.
