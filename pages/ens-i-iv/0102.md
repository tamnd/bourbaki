The relation $ (\exists x)\left( x \in \bigcup_{i \in I} X_i \text{ and } y \in \Gamma(x) \right) $ is equivalent to

$$
(\exists x)(\exists i)(i \in I \text{ and } x \in X_i \text{ and } y \in \Gamma(x)),
$$

hence to $ (\exists i)(i \in I \text{ and } y \in \Gamma\langle X_i \rangle) $, hence is equivalent to $ y \in \bigcup_{i \in I} \Gamma\langle X_i \rangle $; this proves the first formula. As to the second formula, we have $ \bigcap_{i \in I} X_i \subset X_i $ for all $ i \in I $, whence (\S 3, Proposition 2)

$$
\Gamma\left\langle \bigcap_{i \in I} X_i \right\rangle \subset \Gamma\langle X_i \rangle,
$$

and consequently

$$
\Gamma\left\langle \bigcap_{i \in I} X_i \right\rangle \subset \bigcap_{i \in I} \Gamma\langle X_i \rangle.
$$

¶ If $ \Gamma $ is an arbitrary correspondence (and in particular an arbitrary function), the formula

$$
\Gamma\left\langle \bigcap_{i \in I} X_i \right\rangle = \bigcap_{i \in I} \Gamma\langle X_i \rangle
$$

is usually *false*.

\* For example, in the plane $ \mathbf{R}^2 $ the first projections of the lines $ y = x $ and $ y = x + 1 $ are equal to $ \mathbf{R} $, but the intersection of these lines is empty, and therefore so is the first projection of this intersection (*). \*

However, we have the following important result :

PROPOSITION 4. *Let f be a mapping of A into B and let $ (Y_i)_{i \in I} $ be a family of subsets of B. Then $ \overline{f}^{-1}\left\langle \bigcap_{i \in I} Y_i \right\rangle = \bigcap_{i \in I} \overline{f}^{-1}\langle Y_i \rangle $.*

To prove this, let $ x $ be an element of $ \bigcap_{i \in I} \overline{f}^{-1}\langle Y_i \rangle $. We have $ f(x) \in Y_i $ for all $ i \in I $, whence $ f(x) \in \bigcap_{i \in I} Y_i $, and consequently

$$
x \in \overline{f}^{-1}\left\langle \bigcap_{i \in I} Y_i \right\rangle.
$$

(*) A celebrated error arising from the application of this formula is that committed by H. Lebesgue in his attempt to prove that the projection on an axis of a Borel set in the plane is again a Borel set (this statement was subsequently shown to be incorrect, and the discussion it provoked was the origin of the theory of "Souslin" sets) : Lebesgue asserted that the projection of the intersection of a decreasing sequence of sets is equal to the intersection of their projections (*Journal de Mathématiques, (6) **1** (1905), pp. 191-192).*
