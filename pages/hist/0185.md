In its simplest geometric form, integration by parts consists in writing the area bounded by $ Ox, Oy $ and an arc of the monotonic curve $ y = f(x) $ joining a point $(a, 0)$ of $ Ox $ to a point $(0, b)$ of $ Oy $ as $ \int_0^a y dx = \int_0^b x dy $; and it is frequently used in an implicit way. With Pascal ([244], v. IX, pp. 17-18) appears the following generalisation, already much better hidden: $ f(x) $ being as above, let $ g(x) $ be a function $ \geq 0 $, and let $ G(x) = \int_0^x g(x) dx $; then one has $ \int_0^a y g(x) dx = \int_0^b G(x) dy $, which he proves ingeniously by evaluating in two ways the volume of the solid $ 0 \leq x \leq a, 0 \leq y \leq f(x), 0 \leq z \leq g(x) $; the particular case $ g(x) = x^n, G(x) = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} $ plays an important role, both with Pascal (loc. cit., pp. 19-21) and with Fermat ([109], v. I, p. 271); this latter (whose work carries the significant title of "Transmutation and emendation of equations of curves, and its varied applications to the comparison of curvilinear spaces between them and with rectilinear spaces ...") does not prove it, no doubt because he judges it useless to repeat what Pascal had just published. These theorems of "transmutation", where we would see a combination of integration by parts and changes of variables, take the place of this to some extent, which is only introduced very late; it is in effect contrary to the way of thinking, still too geometric and too little analytic, of the time, to allow the use of variables other than those imposed by the figure, that is to say one or other of the co-ordinates (or sometimes polar co-ordinates) then the arc of the curve.
