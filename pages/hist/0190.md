For the remainder, and although he was soon in possession of much more general methods for expansions in power series, such as the method of Newton’s polygon so-called (for algebraic functions) ([198 d], p. 221) and that of indeterminate coefficients, he returns often in what follows, with a kind of predilection, to the binomial series and to its generalisations; and it is from there, for example, that he seems to have drawn out the expansion of $ \int x^\alpha (1 + x)^\beta dx $ which was referred to above ([198 d], p. 209).

The evolution of ideas on the continent, meanwhile, is very different, and much more abstract. Pascal had drawn close to Fermat in the study of binomial coefficients (from which he constructs what he calls the “arithmetic triangle”) and their use in the calculation of probabilities and in the calculation of differences; when he tackles integration, he introduces there the same ideas. Like his predecessors, when he uses the language of indivisibles, he conceives of the integral $ F(x) = \int_0^x f(x)dx $ as the value of the ratio of the “sum of all the ordinates of the curve”

$$
S \left( f \left( \frac{n}{N} \right) \right) = \sum_{0 \leq p < Nx} f \left( \frac{p}{N} \right),
$$

to the “unit” $ N = \sum_{0 \leq p < N} 1 $ for $ N = \infty $ ([244], v. VIII, pp. 352-355) (or, when he abandons this language for the correct language of exhaustion, as a limit of this ratio for $ N $ growing indefinitely). But, having problems about moments in view, he observes that, when it is a matter of discrete masses $ y_i $ spread out at equidistant intervals, the calculation of the total mass reduces to the operation $ S y_n $ defined above, and the calculation of the moment to the operation $ S^2 y_n $; and, by analogy, he iterates the operation $ \int $ to form what he calls the “triangular sum of the ordinates”, thus, in our language, the limits of the sums $ N^{-2} S^2 \left( f \left( \frac{n}{N} \right) \right) $, that is to say the integrals $ F_2(x) = \int_0^x F(x)dx $; a new iteration gives him the “pyramidal sums” $ F_3(x) = \int_0^x F_2(x)dx $, limits of $ N^{-3} S^3 \left( f \left( \frac{n}{N} \right) \right) $; the context besides is sufficient to show that, if he stops there, it is not because of a lack of generality in his thought nor in his language, but solely because he only expects to use these, whose systematic employment is at the basis of a good part of his results, and of which he proves immediately the properties that we would write as

$$
F_2(x) = \int_0^x (x - u)f(u)du, \quad F_3 = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^x (x - u)^2 f(u)du
$$

([244], v. VIII, pp. 361-367); all that without writing a single formula, but in a language so transparent and so precise that one can immediately transcribe it into formulae as has just been done.
