If $R(X)$ is the minimal polynomial (unitary and with rational integer coefficients) of any one of these "periods" $\eta$, Kummer, basing himself on the formulae of Gauss, proves that, over the field $\mathbf{F}_q$, $R(X)$ is still decomposed into distinct factors of the first degree $X - u_j$ ($1 \leq j \leq e$), and it is to each of these $u_j$ that he associates this time a "prime ideal factor" $q_j$. In order to define "divisibility by $q_j$", Kummer writes every $x \in \mathbf{Z}[\zeta]$ in the form $x = \sum_{k=0}^{f-1} \zeta^k y_k$, where each $y_k \in K$ is itself written in a unique way as a polynomial of degree $\leq e-1$ in $\eta$, with rational integer coefficients; he says that $x$ is divisible by $q_j$ if and only if, when $u_j$ is substituted for $\eta$ in each of the $y_k$, the elements of $\mathbf{F}_q$ obtained are *all* zero. But it was still necessary to define the "exponent" of $q_j$ in $x$. For that, Kummer introduces what we would now call a *uniformiser* for $q_j$, that is to say an element $\rho_j \in K$ such that $N(\rho_j) \equiv 0 \pmod{q}, N(\rho_j) \not\equiv 0 \pmod{q^2}$, and finally such that $\rho_j$ is divisible by $q_j$ (in the sense defined above) but by *none other* of the ideal factors $\neq q_j$ of $q$. The existence of such a $\rho_j$ had been substantially proved by Kronecker in his dissertation the year before ([186 a], v. I, p. 23); then setting $\rho_j' = N(\rho_j)/\rho_j$, Kummer says that the exponent of $q_j$ in $x$ is equal to $h$ if one has $x \rho_{j'}^h \equiv 0 \pmod{q^h}$, but $x \rho_{j'}^{h+1} \not\equiv 0 \pmod{q^{h+1}}$; he starts of course by proving that the relation $x \rho_j' \equiv 0 \pmod{q}$ is equivalent to the fact that $x$ is divisible by $q_j$ (in the previous sense). Once these definitions are set up, the extension to $\mathbf{Z}[\zeta]$ of the usual laws of divisibility for the "ideal numbers" did not offer any serious problems; and from his first memoir [188 a] Kummer was even able, by using the "method of drawers" of Dirichlet, to prove that the "classes" of "ideal factors" were *finite* in number.\footnote{He only takes up there an argument from Kronecker's dissertation, referring to classes of solutions of equations of the form $N(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}) = a$ ([188 a], v. II, p. 25). On the other hand Kummer often alludes to results that Dirichlet would have obtained about equations of this type (for an arbitrary field of algebraic numbers); but these results have not been published, nor found in the papers of Dirichlet.}
