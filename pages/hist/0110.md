The same year 1882 also sees the appearance of the great memoir of Kronecker awaited for more than 20 years ([186 a], v. II, pp. 237-387). Much more ambitious than the work of Dedekind-Weber, it is unhappily also much more vague and obscure. His central theme is (in modern language) the study of the ideals of a finite integral algebra over one of the rings of polynomials $\mathbf{C}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ or $\mathbf{Z}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$; Kronecker limits himself $a$ priori to those of these ideals that are of finite type (the fact that they are all of this kind was only to be proved (for the ideals of $\mathbf{C}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ several years later by Hilbert during his work on invariants ([163 a], v. II, pp. 199-257)). As far as $\mathbf{C}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ or $\mathbf{Z}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ are concerned one was naturally led to associate to each ideal of one of these rings the "algebraic variety" consisting of the common zeros of all the elements of the ideal; and the geometrical studies in 2 or 3 dimensions done during the course of the XIXth century would intuitively lead to the idea that every variety is the union of "irreducible" varieties, finite in number, whose "dimensions" are not necessarily the same. It appears that the proof of this fact was the goal that Kronecker was setting himself, although he does not state it explicitly anywhere, and that nowhere in his memoir can any definition of "irreducible variety" nor of "dimension" be found. $^{20}$ By a linear change of co-ordinates, one can assume that the generators $F_i$ ($1 \leq r \leq r$) of the ideal are polynomials where the term of highest degree in $X_1$ is of the form $c_i X_1^{m_i}$, where $c_i$ is a constant $\neq 0$. It can also be assumed that the $F_i$ do not have a common factor. One considers then for $2r$ indeterminates $u_i, v_i$ ($1 \leq i \leq r$) the polynomials $\sum_{i=1}^r u_i F_i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^r v_i F_I$ as polynomials in $X_i$; one calculates their Sylvester resultant, which is a polynomial in the $u_i$ and $v_i$, with coefficients in $\mathbf{C}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ (respectively $\mathbf{Z}[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$); in cancelling these coefficients, a system of equations is obtained whose solutions $(x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ are exactly the projections of the solutions $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ of the system of equations $F_i(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = 0$ ($1 \leq i \leq r$). One can then continue with the application of the method by induction on $n$.
