But these questions also have a purely theoretical and even arithmetical aspect. Let us agree to denote by $\Delta' x_n$ the sequences of successive differences of a sequence $(x_n)_{n \in \mathbf{N}}$, defined by recurrence by means of $\Delta x_n = x_{n+1} - x_n$, and

$$
\Delta' x_n = \Delta ({\Delta'}^{-1} x_n),
$$

and to denote by $S'$ the inverse operation of $\Delta$ and its iterates, thus putting $y_n = S.x_n$ if $y_0 = 0, \Delta y_n = x_n$, and $S'.x_n = S({S'}^{-1} x_n)$; one has $S' x_n = \sum_{p=0}^{n-r} \binom{n-p-1}{r-1} x_p$,

and, in particular, if $x_n = 1$ for all $n$, one has $S.x_n = n$, and the sequences $S^2 x_n$ and $S^3 x_n$ are those of the "triangular" and "pyramidal" numbers already studied by the Greek arithmeticians, and in general $S^r x_n = \binom{n}{r}$ for $n \geq r$ (and $S' x_n = 0$ for $n < r$); these sequences were introduced, from this point of view, certainly as early as the XVI$^{th}$ century; they appeared of their own accord also in the combinatorial problems, which, either on their own, or in connection with probabilities, played rather a large rôle in the mathematics of the XVII$^{th}$ century, for example, with Fermat and Pascal, then with Leibniz. They also appeared in the expression for the sum of the $m^{th}$ powers of the first N integers, whose calculation, as we have seen, underlay the integration of $\int x^m dx$ for $m$ an integer, by the first method of Fermat ((XI), v. II, p. 83).
