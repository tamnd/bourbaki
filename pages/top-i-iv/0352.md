If $x, y, z, t$ are points of $\overline{\mathbf{R}}$ such that $x \leq y$ and $z \leq t$, then $x + z \leq y + t$ whenever both sides of this inequality are defined.

Note that in $\overline{\mathbf{R}}$ the relation $x < y$ implies $x + z < y + z$ only if $z$ is finite, by formulae (2); it is easily verified that the relations $x < y$ and $z < t$ imply $x + z < y + t$ whenever both sides of this inequality are defined.

In $\overline{\mathbf{R}}$ we put $x^+ = \sup (x, 0), x^- = \sup (-x, 0), |x| = \sup (x, -x)$; thus $(+\infty)^+ = (-\infty)^- = +\infty$ and $(+\infty)^- = (-\infty)^+ = 0$, and $|+\infty| = |- \infty| = +\infty$. The sums $x^+ - x^-$ and $x^+ + x^-$ are defined for all $x \in \overline{\mathbf{R}}$ and are therefore equal to $x$ and $|x|$ respectively by the principle of extension of identities. Also, whenever the sum $x + y$ is defined, we have $|x + y| \leq |x| + |y|$.

Note on the contrary that formulae (6) and (7) of § 1, no. 1 may no longer make sense for certain values of $x$ and $y$ in $\overline{\mathbf{R}}$; for example, if $x = -\infty$ and $y = 0$ we have $\sup (x, y) = 0$, but the sum $x + (y - x)^+$ is not defined, because $(y - x)^+ = +\infty$.
